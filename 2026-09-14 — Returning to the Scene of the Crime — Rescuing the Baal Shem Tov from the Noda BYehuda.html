חֲזָרָה לִמְקוֹם הַחֵטְא
Returning to the Scene of the Crime
Rescuing the Ba’al Shem Tov from the Noda BiYehuda
A Shiur by Rabbi Yisroel Kraz · Elul 5786
Recorded: Elul 5786 · Prepared: 2 Tishrei 5787 / September 14, 2026

The gemara in Yoma 86b sets a startling test for a true ba’al teshuvah: he is placed a second time in the very scene of his fall — the same woman, the same place, the same season — and this time he walks away. Around this one line a whole architecture of dispute has grown up. The Rambam and Rabbeinu Yonah read it as a scene the Ribbono shel Olam arranges from Above. Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid warns against dragging oneself into a nisayon. The Kli Yakar and Rav Eliyahu HaKohen of Izmir read it altogether literally — the ba’al teshuvah is required to return, physically, to the place of his sin. The Ba’al Shem Tov, in a stunning ma’amar preserved in his name, presses further still. And the Noda BiYehuda and Rav Tzadok HaKohen, thundering in the opposite direction, brand the whole approach bavaa maasios — the stuff of fables. This shiur traces the machlokes to its edge, then rescues the Ba’al Shem Tov by way of the Mitteler Rebbe’s dream and the Ramo MiPano’s reading of teshuvah me’ahavah.

I. The Gemara in Yoma — What Is a Ba’al Teshuvah?

We begin with the source that anchors everything that follows. The gemara in Yoma 86b asks the deceptively simple question: how does one identify a ba’al teshuvah? Not a person who has merely repented, notes the Rav, but one who has mastered the teshuvah — ba’al in the sense of ownership. He has reached what the Rambam will call teshuvah gemurah.1

אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה: כְּגוֹן שֶׁבָּא לְיָדוֹ דְּבַר עֲבֵירָה פַּעַם רִאשׁוֹנָה וּשְׁנִיָּה וְנִצַּל הֵימֶנָּה. מַחֲוֵי רַב יְהוּדָה: בְּאוֹתָהּ אִשָּׁה, בְּאוֹתוֹ פֶּרֶק, בְּאוֹתוֹ מָקוֹם. — Yoma 86b (source sheet #1)  1

Three parameters — the same woman, the same perek, the same place. Rav Yehudah’s hand-gesture makes the demand literal. The perek, as we will see, is read two ways: the same season of the year, or the same season of life — the years in which the ta’avah still burns. The place is not incidental either. In one street you felt secure enough to stumble; in another you would not have dared. For the reenactment to count, every switch must be flipped exactly as it was the first time.

And then the obvious objection: does the gemara really mean that a person should go back and place himself in the crosshairs? The Rav quotes the Leket Yosher, a sefer from roughly six hundred years ago, which reports the case of a yid who took Rav Yehudah at his word, returned to the same scene — and stumbled again. On its face, the gemara’s advice is dangerous. The rest of the shiur is a running argument over what to do with it.2

II. The Rambam — Teshuvah Gemurah and Yodei’a Ta’alumos

The Rambam opens Hilchos Teshuvah 2:1 with the framing that will govern the rest of the debate:

אֵיזוֹ הִיא תְּשׁוּבָה גְמוּרָה? זֶה שֶׁבָּא לְיָדוֹ דָּבָר שֶׁעָבַר בּוֹ, וְאֶפְשָׁר בְּיָדוֹ לַעֲשׂוֹתוֹ, וּפֵרַשׁ וְלֹא עָשָׂה מִפְּנֵי הַתְּשׁוּבָה, לֹא מִיִּרְאָה וְלֹא מִכִּשְׁלוֹן כֹּחַ. כֵּיצַד? הֲרֵי שֶׁבָּא עַל אִשָּׁה בַּעֲבֵירָה וּלְאַחַר זְמַן נִתְיַחֵד עִמָּהּ וְהוּא עוֹמֵד בְּאַהֲבָתוֹ בָּהּ וּבְכֹחַ גּוּפוֹ וּבַמְּדִינָה שֶׁעָבַר בָּהּ, וּפֵרַשׁ וְלֹא עָבַר — זֶהוּ בַּעַל תְּשׁוּבָה גְמוּרָה. — Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah 2:1 (source #2)  3

The Rav dwells on a phrase every reader glides past: she’ba l’yado — when the thing came his way. The Rambam does not say she’yechapes achareha. He is not sending the penitent out to search her out. He is describing what happens when the Aibershter Himself arranges the second encounter and the person, standing in it, walks away. And it is by no means limited to arayos — the Ba’al Shem Tov will apply the very same rule to lashon hara. A true teshuvah is one you know would hold up under the exact conditions in which it broke.

In the next halachah, the Rambam gives the second reading — the one the acharonim treat as a distinct pshat in Rav Yehudah’s statement:

וְיָעִיד עָלָיו יוֹדֵעַ תַּעֲלוּמוֹת שֶׁלֹּא יָשׁוּב לְזֶה הַחֵטְא לְעוֹלָם, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר וְלֹא נֹאמַר עוֹד אֱלֹקֵינוּ לְמַעֲשֵׂה יָדֵינוּ. — Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah 2:2 (source #3)  3

Here the reenactment need not occur in the world at all. It occurs in the eye of the Yodei’a Ta’alumos: the Aibershter peers into the recesses of the heart and testifies that, were this person placed in that street, in that room, at that hour, he would abstain. The Rav frames it as the general definition — teshuvah stam — while the physical reenactment of 2:1 is teshuvah gemurah, the higher grade.

Why, then, would the Aibershter ever bring the actual scene about, if He already sees in the heart that the person would not fall? The Sfas Emes, in his commentary on the sugya, offers a beautiful answer: not for Heaven’s sake, but for ours. A person can eat himself alive with doubt long after his teshuvah is real; he replays the sin and second-guesses his own resolve. The Aibershter stages the reenactment so that the penitent himself should know, past all self-suspicion, that his teshuvah stood. Only then can he pick up and move.4

III. The Meiri — The Mikvah and the Sheretz

The Meiri — Rabbeinu Menachem Meiri of Perpignan — wrote an entire sefer on teshuvah, the Chibbur HaTeshuvah, and he raises the question that haunts every honest penitent: what about the day after the teshuvah, when the person falls again? Does the fall retroactively invalidate the repentance?

שֶׁבְּעֵת הַתְּשׁוּבָה גּוֹמֵר בְּלִבּוֹ לַעֲזוֹב דַּרְכֵי הָרַע תַּכְלִית עֲזִיבָה, אֲבָל אַחַר כֵּן יַכְרִיחֵהוּ יִצְרוֹ לָשׁוּב לַחֲטוֹא… שֶׁזֶּה אָמְנָם כְּבָר הָיְתָה תְּשׁוּבָתוֹ תְּשׁוּבָה בְּעֵת הֱיוֹתָהּ עֲשׂוּיָה, וְהוֹעִילָה לוֹ עַל חֲטָאָיו הַמֻּקְדָּמִים. וְהָיָה זֶה בְּדִמְיוֹן מִי שֶׁטָּבַל וְאַחַר הַטְּבִילָה עָלָה שֶׁרֶץ בְּיָדוֹ — לֹא שֶׁיִּתְפּוֹס שֶׁרֶץ בְּיָדוֹ, שֶׁכְּבָר הָיְתָה טְבִילָתוֹ טְבִילָה וְהֶעֱבִירָה טֻמְאָה קוֹדֶמֶת. — Meiri, Chibbur HaTeshuvah (source #5)  5

The mashal is precise. A man who goes into a mikvah holding a sheretz gains nothing; his tumah travels in with him. But a man who tovels cleanly, and then, minutes after emerging, touches a sheretz — his tevilah stood. He is tamei again, but for a new cause. So too the teshuvah of the moment: if in that moment the resolve was total, the teshuvah was real. Tomorrow’s fall is tomorrow’s account.

The Rav noted the near-identity between the Meiri’s chiddush and the Alter Rebbe’s definition of a beinoni: a person is a beinoni if, at this moment, he would never sin again — even if tomorrow he does. The beinoni lives one day at a time.

IV. Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid and Rabbeinu Yonah — Do Not Seek Her Out

The Sefer Chassidim (source #4) forbids the literal reading outright. A person should not say, “Rav Yehudah requires the same woman, the same place, the same perek — therefore let me go find her.”

אַל יֹאמַר אָדָם: הֲרֵי אָמַר רַב יְהוּדָה בְּאוֹתָהּ אִשָּׁה בְּאוֹתוֹ מָקוֹם בְּאוֹתוֹ פֶּרֶק, אֲקַיֵּים זֶה. שֶׁמָּא הָאִשָּׁה תַּחְפֹּץ לְהִזָּדֵק וְיִהְיֶה לוֹ חֵטְא. וְלֹא אָמְרוּ שֶׁיָּבִיא אָדָם אֶת עַצְמוֹ לִידֵי חֵטְא. — Sefer Chassidim, siman 165 (source #4)  6

Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid’s worry is telling. He is not concerned only for the penitent’s own resolve. He is concerned for the woman: even if you mean it, the other party may not; and she will draw him in.

Rabbeinu Yonah, in Sha’arei Teshuvah, gives the same two-tiered picture as the Rambam. In his enumeration of the ingredients of teshuvah, ingredient nineteen is azivas ha’chet b’hizdammen lo — abandoning the sin when it comes his way. If the Aibershter arranges the encounter and the person, with his ta’avah intact, abstains, that is the peak. But if the encounter does not come, one adds fear of Heaven day by day until Bochen levavos hu yavin — the One who searches hearts recognizes that were the nisayon to arrive, the person would stand.7

The Rav paused here to tell the story of Rabbeinu Yonah’s own teshuvah — his role in the campaign against the Rambam and the burning of the Moreh Nevuchim in Montpellier, his shock when, in the same square, twenty-four wagon-loads of seforim were later burned by the Christians, and his vow to travel to Teveryah, to the Rambam’s kever, with a minyan. He died in Toledo before he could reach it. Sha’arei Teshuvah was, in the tradition, written under the weight of that guilt.

V. The Kli Yakar — A Whopper on Parah Adumah

Here the ship starts to rock. The Kli Yakar — Rabbi Shlomo Ephraim of Luntschitz, a talmid of the Maharal and a great darshan in Prague — reads Rav Yehudah’s statement altogether literally. And he grounds it in the very paradox of parah adumah.

וְעַל צַד הָרֶמֶז מָקוֹם אִתִּי לוֹמַר עַל פִּי דִבְרֵי בַּעַל הָעֲקֵדָה שֶׁפֵּרֵשׁ כָּל עִנְיְנֵי הַפָּרָה עַל דַּרְכֵי הַתְּשׁוּבָה… שֶׁרְפוּאַת הַנֶּפֶשׁ כִּרְפוּאַת הַגּוּף. כִּי הַמַּשְׁקֶה הַמּוֹעִיל לִתְרוּפַת אָדָם חוֹלֶה מַזִּיק לְאָדָם בָּרִיא… כִּי מְקוֹם הַחֵטְא וְהַשַּׁעַר שֶׁעָבַר בּוֹ — שָׁם צָרִיךְ שֶׁתְּהֵא גְבוּרָתוֹ, שֶׁכְּבָר טָעַם טַעַם עֲבֵירָה וּמְבַקֵּשׁ לִמּוּדוֹ. — Kli Yakar, Bamidbar 19 (source #7)  8

The medicine for the ill is poison for the healthy, said Plato; the same is true, says the Kli Yakar, in the spiritual realm. A ta’anis is a curative for one gavra and a defilement for another. So too the return to the scene: for the tzaddik it is a forbidden yichud, a nisayon one may not bring upon oneself; for the ba’al teshuvah it is the very act of purification. That, he says, is the sod of the pasuk in Hoshea:

וְהָיָה בִּמְקוֹם אֲשֶׁר יֵאָמֵר לָהֶם לֹא עַמִּי אַתֶּם, יֵאָמֵר לָהֶם בְּנֵי אֵל חָי. Not instead — bimekom means in the very same physical place. In the very room where six months ago the Aibershter said, Lo ami atem, in that very room He now says, Bnei Kel chai. And the Kli Yakar presses further into the gemara in Berachos: mekom she’ba’alei teshuvah omdim, tzaddikim gemurim einam yecholim la’amod. Not a metaphor of spiritual altitude — the physical makom. The ba’al teshuvah is required to stand in yichud with that very woman; a tzaddik gamur is forbidden to.8

VI. Rav Eliyahu HaKohen of Izmir — and Reb Meir of Premishlan

The same reading appears in Ezer Eliyahu of Rav Eliyahu HaKohen of Izmir — the author of the Shevet Mussar and Midrash Talpiyos, born 1659. He supports it with a fresh source: the gemara in Berachos 19a which says that if one sees a talmid chacham sin at night, one must not think ill of him by day, for he has surely done teshuvah. Rav Eliyahu HaKohen reads the gemara radically:

אִם רָאִיתָ תַּלְמִיד חָכָם שֶׁעָבַר עֲבֵירָה בַּלַּיְלָה — אַל תְּהַרְהֵר אַחֲרָיו בַּיּוֹם. וְהַכַּוָּונָה, שֶׁמָּה שֶׁרָאִיתָ הוּא בְעַצְמוֹ הָיָה מַעֲשֵׂה הַתְּשׁוּבָה, שֶׁחָזַר לִמְקוֹם הַחֵטְא לְקַיֵּים דִּבְרֵי רַב יְהוּדָה. — Ezer Eliyahu (source #7, continued)  9

Meaning: what you saw — the talmid chacham walking into the harlot’s house — was not a sin followed by teshuvah. It was the teshuvah. He was returning to the scene of a fall of twenty-five years earlier, this time to walk out.

The Rav paired this with the story of Rav Yosef Shaul Nathanson — the future rav of Lvov — who as a young man visited Rav Meir of Premishlan, looked in through the doorway, and saw the rebbe apparently “wasting time,” staring at the wall. It troubled him deeply. Before he left, Rav Meir said to him: “You know that gemara that if you see a talmid chacham sinning, he is really doing teshuvah? It means — you walk past someone’s room and you see a talmid chacham sitting at the table not learning; you think he is wasting time. No. He is doing teshuvah.”

VII. The Semak, the Forest, and the Tashbetz’s Chiddush

The Semak — Rabbi Yitzchak of Corbeil, one of the Ba’alei Tosafos — brings the reading in through a back door. His question in siman 3 is whether, in a case of ordinary aveiros where the halachah is ya’avor v’al yehareig, one is nevertheless allowed to give one’s life. He answers yes, provided kavanaso l’shem Shamayim, and proves it from a story of Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid.

The Rav retold the story with the detail the source sheet quietly reproduces (source #8–9). Students of Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid — or, in the version of the Ramo MiPano preserved in Kanfei Yonah, students of Rabbeinu Yonah — went out into a place of wild animals in the desert to test whether they could deploy shemos ha’kodesh. It worked. In the morning their rebbe heard, and decreed: to atone for the misuse of the shemos, they were to return to the very place, this time without invoking the shemos, and give themselves over. They did. They were devoured. What was found in the morning were their hands and their faces — the very organs that had held and read the sacred names, untouched.10

Why send them back at all? Why not have them repent at home? The Tashbetz — Rav Shimshon ben Tzemach Duran of Algiers, one of the last of the Rishonim — asks exactly that kushya and answers with our theme:

דְּעַל כָּרְחָךְ צָרִיךְ שֶׁתְּהֵא תְּשׁוּבָתוֹ בְּאוֹתוֹ מָקוֹם וּבְאוֹתוֹ פֶּרֶק וּבְאוֹתוֹ עִנְיָן שֶׁחָטָא, כִּדְאָמְרִינַן בְּיוֹמָא — שֶׁלֹּא תִּתָּכֵן הַתְּשׁוּבָה הַשְּׁלֵמָה אֶלָּא בְּמָקוֹם שֶׁעָבַר. — Tashbetz (paraphrased on the sheet), on the Semak’s story  10

So the Semak, the Kli Yakar, the Shevet Mussar and the Tashbetz all converge on the literal reading: complete teshuvah demands the reenactment.

VIII. The Ba’al Shem Tov — Reish Lakish and the Lud’ai

And then comes the ma’amar of the Ba’al Shem Tov, preserved in his name in Ba’al Shem Tov al HaTorah, quoted also by Rav Yeiva Sar and by the Komarna. The Ba’al Shem Tov reads the gemara in Gittin — Reish Lakish selling himself to a group of lud’ai (cannibals) with a hidden weapon — against the reading of Tosafos. Tosafos assumed the incident occurred before Reish Lakish’s teshuvah, when he was still a bandit. The Ba’al Shem Tov insists it happened after: precisely because he had risen, he was obligated to return to the scene, to the same class of people among whom he had sinned, and this time to destroy them.11

וְאָמַר מוֹרֵנוּ הַבַּעַל שֵׁם טוֹב זַ"ל: רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ זַבֵּן נַפְשֵׁיהּ לְלוּדָאֵי, כִּי שָׁמַע בִּמְתִיבְתָּא דִרְקִיעָא שֶׁהִקְשׁוּ עַל הַגְּמָרָא דְּיוֹמָא דְּתְשׁוּבָה צְרִיכָה בְּאוֹתָהּ אִשָּׁה בְּאוֹתוֹ מָקוֹם, הֵיכִי יִהְיֶה זֶה — דְּהָא כְּשֶׁנִּכְנָס לְשֵׁם תְּשׁוּבָה נִכְנָס, בְּהַכְנָעָה וּבְלֵב נִשְׁבָּר, וְלָאו כְּפַעַם הָרִאשׁוֹנָה. וְאָמְרוּ בִּמְתִיבְתָּא דְּהִתִּירוּ לוֹ לִיכָּנֵס בְּעֹז וּבְעֹז הַתַּאֲוָה כְּמוֹ בַּפַּעַם הָרִאשׁוֹנָה, וְאָז יִכְפֶּה אֶת יִצְרוֹ וְלֹא יַעֲבֹר עַל רְצוֹן הַשֵּׁם, וּבָזֶה — וְסָר עֲוֹנֶךָ וְחַטָּאתְךָ תְּכֻפָּר. — Ba’al Shem Tov al HaTorah (source #10)  11

The Ba’al Shem Tov is grappling with a genuine problem in the literal reading. If the second entry into the harlot’s house is done with lev nishbar, in humility and with the express intention to abstain, then it is not, in truth, a reenactment at all. The test has been rigged from Above. The ba’al teshuvah is walking in already braced. Where is the ta’avah against which he is meant to prevail?

The answer he brings down from the Mesivta d’Rekiya: the ba’al teshuvah is authorized — hitiru lo — to walk in with the full force of the ta’avah he had the first time. Not the broken man; the same man, with the same fire. And only then, at the crucial instant, does he restrain his yetzer. This is what turns the encounter into a genuine reenactment, and this is what accomplishes v’sar avonecha. And it applies, the Ba’al Shem Tov says, to every aveirah — lashon hara included. He grounds it in a Yerushalmi that puts the aphorism chata’im tirdof ra’ah into an unexpected voice: not that evil pursues the sinner, but that the (repentant) sinner is to pursue the evil — not to do it, but to place himself in its presence and restrain his yetzer.

IX. The Noda BiYehuda — Bavaa Ma’asios!

Enter the Noda BiYehuda — Rav Yechezkel Landau, rav of Prague in the second half of the eighteenth century — who in one of his drashos writes down the whole reading and dismisses it in a single line: bavaa ma’asios. Grandmothers’ tales. To place oneself in yichud in order to test oneself? Impossible. And he presses a kal vachomer with real force:

וּמָה צַדִּיק גָּמוּר שֶׁלֹּא חָטָא מֵעוֹלָם אָסוּר לוֹ לְהָבִיא עַצְמוֹ לִידֵי נִסָּיוֹן — שֶׁכֵּן דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ הִתְפַּלֵּל "בְּחָנֵנִי ה' וְנַסֵּנִי" וְנִכְשַׁל בְּבַת שֶׁבַע — קַל וָחֹמֶר הַבַּעַל תְּשׁוּבָה שֶׁכְּבָר טָעַם טַעַם עֲבֵירָה, אֵיךְ יַחְזִיר עַצְמוֹ לִמְקוֹם הַנִּסָּיוֹן? — Noda BiYehuda, drush (source #11)  12

David HaMelech, before he had ever sinned, asked to be tested — and stumbled at Bas Sheva. The gemara in Sanhedrin draws the mussar from that: even a tzaddik may not ask for a nisayon. If so, how much more so a ba’al teshuvah who has already tasted the sin. Who is less trustworthy at the door of the tavern — the man who has never drunk, or the man in recovery? Obviously the second. To send him back to the scene is bad advice.

X. Rav Tzadok HaKohen — the Same Kushya, Twice

Rav Tzadok HaKohen of Lublin, in Takanas HaShavim, raises exactly the same kushya in exactly the same terms. He acknowledges — parenthetically — that Rav Yeiva Sar, in his peirush on Tehillim, cites the Ba’al Shem Tov’s reading, and that he has heard of a Kli Yakar in Chukas which says the like, though he does not have the sefer in front of him. He does not deny the sources. He simply says: it cannot be. It contradicts common sense, and it contradicts Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid, and it contradicts the Leket Yosher’s reported casualty. He is willing to concede only the Yerushalmi’s picture of David HaMelech testing himself with his pilagshim — but there, notes Rav Tzadok, the worst-case outcome is a permitted bi’ah. That is not a reenactment; that is a controlled exercise.13

XI. The Mitteler Rebbe’s Dream — and Teshuvah Me’Ahavah vs. Me’Yirah

To resolve the fight, the Rav turned to a story recorded by the Frierdiker Rebbe and quoted many times by the Rebbe.14 The Mitteler Rebbe once dreamt of a raging river spanned by a narrow plank. Three great rabbanim came to cross. Two — whom he did not recognize — tried and could not; the plank shook under them. The third, his own father, the Alter Rebbe, walked across as though the plank were solid. When he woke, he described the two others to the Alter Rebbe. “One,” said the Alter Rebbe, “was the Ba’al Shem Tov. One was the Maggid of Mezritch. And the reason I was able to cross is that I deal with ba’alei teshuvah.”

The Rebbe’s reading: the river is the raging mayim ha’zeidonim, the currents of temptation. A tzaddik who has never sinned, confronted by them, is not guaranteed to hold. He may stumble — David HaMelech is the case in point. But the ba’al teshuvah, who has tasted the sin and is now repulsed by it, has the fixed compass of a veteran. He is more trustworthy than the tzaddik, not less.

Which is directly the reverse of what the Alter Rebbe himself teaches, in the name of his own rebbe the Maggid, on the Rashi at the end of Kedoshim. There, a person walking past treif food is told to say “I want it, but the Aibershter said no.” The Maggid ruled: this advice applies only to a tzaddik — someone who has never eaten there. A ba’al teshuvah in that domain must not say ipshi; his desire is still live, and he will fall. On that reading the ba’al teshuvah is trusted less, and the tzaddik more.

So which is it? The Rebbe’s answer, in a footnote to a sichah: it depends on the kind of teshuvah.

  • Teshuvah me’yirah — the person still wants the aveirah; he is only kept from it by fear of Heaven. His passions remain in the old channel. This is the person the Maggid speaks of: he must stay far away.
  • Teshuvah me’ahavah — the person’s passions have migrated. He now loves the Aibershter. Ohavei Hashem sin’u ra: the more he loves the Aibershter, the more he hates the aveirah. This is the person of the Mitteler Rebbe’s dream. He is more secure than the tzaddik, and he — and only he — is the one the literal reading of Yoma 86b concerns.

XII. Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Abahu — The Ramo MiPano and the Shlah

The concluding gemara in Berachos 34b (source #12) sets Rabbi Yochanan against Rabbi Abahu: is the tzaddik gamur greater than the ba’al teshuvah, or the reverse? Rabbi Yochanan cites ayin lo ra’asah Elokim zulasecha for the tzaddik gamur. Rabbi Abahu answers with shalom shalom la’rachok v’la’karov — the Aibershter greets the distant one first, and only afterward the near one. The Rambam in Hilchos Teshuvah (source #13) pesken’s like Rabbi Abahu — and pointedly amplifies the gemara’s language: not merely that tzaddikim gemurim do not stand in the place of ba’alei teshuvah, but that they are not able to stand there.

The Maharsha, unwilling to say that a person who actually sinned is on higher ground than one who never did, reads the gemara as being about two people who have both never sinned — a tzaddik without desire, and a beinoni who has desire and vanquishes it. The Ramo MiPano and the Shlah HaKadosh — and the Rebbe cites this at length in letters — disagree. There is no machlokes. Rabbi Yochanan is speaking of teshuvah me’yirah; Rabbi Abahu is speaking of teshuvah me’ahavah. A ba’al teshuvah me’ahavah stands where a tzaddik gamur cannot.15

Which is the resolution of everything the Rav has built. When the gemara in Yoma calls its subject a ba’al teshuvah, it is using the term the way Rabbi Abahu uses it — the teshuvah gemurah of the Rambam, which is teshuvah me’ahavah. That is the person the Ba’al Shem Tov has in view. He is not to be judged by the kal vachomer from a tzaddik, because he is stronger than the tzaddik, not weaker. The Noda BiYehuda’s kal vachomer is airtight — for teshuvah me’yirah. It collapses for teshuvah me’ahavah, which is the only teshuvah the gemara in Yoma has been talking about all along.

Does this mean the ba’al teshuvah me’ahavah is literally to enter yichud gamur? No — yichud mamash would remain forbidden. But to place himself in the vicinity of what once undid him, and to walk away, is not only permitted; on the Ba’al Shem Tov’s reading, it is the very substance of what he owes.

Sources, Citations, and Textual Notes — Artscroll dual-layer
1. יוֹמָא פ"ו ע"ב — the paradigm case of ba’al teshuvah. The Rav opened by observing that ba’al teshuvah in the gemara’s usage means one who has mastered repentance, i.e. reached what the Rambam calls teshuvah gemurah. The three parameters of Rav Yehudah’s hand-gesture — b’osah ishah, b’oso perek, b’oso makom — are read two ways in the Rishonim: b’oso perek as the same season of the year (Rashi’s inclination), or the same season of life (Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah 2:1).
2. לֶקֶט יוֹשֶׁר, chelek 2 (Yoreh De’ah) — the man who took Rav Yehudah literally and stumbled again. The Leket Yosher (Rav Yosef ben Moshe, talmid of the Terumas HaDeshen, mid-15th c. Germany) reports the case as a warning against the literal reading. This is the anchoring “experimental” datum cited by both Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid and later the Noda BiYehuda and Rav Tzadok.
3. רַמְבַּ"ם, הִלְכוֹת תְּשׁוּבָה ב:א–ב — the two-tiered definition. Halachah 1 defines teshuvah gemurah as the actual reenactment (which the Rambam carefully phrases as she’ba l’yado — came his way — not she’chippes achareha). Halachah 2 defines the general teshuvah: abandonment of the sin, removal from thought, resolve of the heart, and testimony from the Yodei’a Ta’alumos. The acharonim (Ohr Sameach, and cf. Lechem Mishneh ad loc.) treat these as the two pshatim in Rav Yehudah’s b’osah ishah.
4. שְׂפַת אֱמֶת on Yoma 86b — why the Aibershter stages the reenactment at all. The Sfas Emes’s answer: the reenactment is lo’ li’tzorech Gavohah, but so that the ba’al teshuvah himself can lay down the burden of self-suspicion. Compare the parallel in the Alter Rebbe’s definition of beinoni in Tanya, ch. 12–13, cited by the Rav.
5. מְאִירִי, חִבּוּר הַתְּשׁוּבָה, ma’amar rishon — the tevilah-and-sheretz mashal. Menachem HaMeiri of Perpignan (Provence, 1249–1310). His Chibbur HaTeshuvah is a systematic treatise on repentance separate from his commentary Beis HaBechirah. The mashal distinguishes sharply between (a) holding a sheretz during tevilah — the sin retained in mind during the teshuvah, which invalidates it — and (b) touching a sheretz after emerging, which does not.
6. סֵפֶר חֲסִידִים, סִימָן קסה. Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid’s striking concern is not merely for the ba’al teshuvah’s own resolve but for the woman — shema ha’ishah tachpotz l’hizdakek. The reading here rules out the literal-reenactment approach at the outset. Cf. Sefaria: Sefer_Chasidim.165.
7. רַבֵּינוּ יוֹנָה, שַׁעֲרֵי תְּשׁוּבָה, שַׁעַר א' עִיקָר יט. Ingredient nineteen of complete teshuvah: azivas ha’chet b’hizdammen lo, v’hu b’sokef ta’avaso. Rabbeinu Yonah, like the Rambam, gives a fallback: if the nisayon does not come, one adds fear of Heaven day by day until Bochen levavos hu yavin. The historical aside on the Montpellier seforim-burning of the 1230s and Rabbeinu Yonah’s vow to travel to the Rambam’s kever in Teveryah is preserved in the classical maggidim tradition; the primary source is a letter of Rav Hillel of Verona to R. Yitzchak b’R. Mordechai (Wertheimer, Ginzei Yerushalayim).
8. כְּלִי יָקָר, בַּמִּדְבָּר יט (פרשת חוקת). Rabbi Shlomo Ephraim of Luntschitz (Prague, d. 1619). His chiddush is dual: (i) parah adumah’s paradox — metaher es ha’temeim u’metamei es ha’tehorim — is the schematic of teshuvah: the same act is a chiyuv for the ba’al teshuvah and a ma’aseh assur for the tzaddik; (ii) bimekom asher yei’amer lahem (Hoshea 2:1) is read as the physical makom. The Kli Yakar also reads mekom she’ba’alei teshuvah omdim in Berachos 34b as a physical statement about the room. Sefaria ref: Kli_Yakar,_Numbers.19.
9. רַב אֵלִיָּהוּ הַכֹּהֵן, עֵזֶר אֵלִיָּהוּ; and בְּרָכוֹת יט ע"א on the talmid chacham who sinned at night. Rav Eliyahu HaKohen of Izmir (1659–1729), author also of Shevet Mussar and Midrash Talpiyos. His radical reading of Berachos 19a: the act you witnessed was itself the teshuvah. The Rav paired this with the story of Rav Meir of Premishlan and the young Rav Yosef Shaul Nathanson (later rav of Lvov, author of Sho’el u’Meishiv), preserved in the classical Premishlan literature.
10. סֵפֶר מִצְוֹת קָטָן (סמ"ק), סִימָן ג; and רַבִּי רְאוּבֵן מַרְגָּלִיּוֹת, notes on Sefer Chassidim, citing the רַמָ"ע מִפָּאנוֹ, Kanfei Yonah; and the תַּשְׁבַּ"ץ ad loc. Rav Yitzchak of Corbeil (d. 1280), Ba’alei Tosafos. His use of the “forest” story of Rabbeinu Yehudah HeChassid’s talmidim is as a proof that dying l’shem Shamayim is permitted even where the halachah is ya’avor. The parallel “desert of wild animals” version is preserved in Kanfei Yonah of the Ramo MiPano (Rav Menachem Azaryah of Fano, 1548–1620) and quoted in Rav Reuven Margolios’s notes on Sefer Chassidim. The Tashbetz (Rav Shimshon ben Tzemach Duran of Algiers, d. 1444) reads the whole story as depending on the literal-reenactment reading of Yoma 86b — without which, he says, sending the talmidim back is inexplicable.
11. בַּעַל שֵׁם טוֹב עַל הַתּוֹרָה, פָּרָשַׁת נִצָּבִים, in the name of the Ba’al Shem Tov; on גִּיטִין מז ע"א (Reish Lakish and the lud’ai). Ba’al Shem Tov al HaTorah is a nineteenth-century collection of teachings preserved in the Ba’al Shem Tov’s name via chains of talmidim. The Rav noted that the same ma’amar is quoted by Rav Yeiva Sar (talmid of the Ba’al Shem Tov’s inner circle) in his peirush on Tehillim, and by the Komarna Rebbe. The Ba’al Shem Tov’s reading of Reish Lakish’s sale to the lud’ai (Gittin 47a) as a post-teshuvah return to the scene stands directly against Tosafos ad loc., who assumed the incident preceded Reish Lakish’s rise. The Yerushalmi cited (evil pursued by the sinner) is Peah 1:1 with the classical drasha reversed. Sefaria: Gittin.47a.4.
12. נוֹדַע בִּיהוּדָה — drush of Rav Yechezkel Landau (Prague, 1713–1793). The Rav read the kal vachomer from David HaMelech (Sanhedrin 107a on bechaneini Hashem v’naseini) as the Noda BiYehuda’s central argument. The polemic bavaa ma’asios is characteristic of the Noda BiYehuda’s stance toward what he perceived as the excesses of the emerging Chassidic reading tradition. On the Noda BiYehuda’s well-known tshuvah (Yoreh De’ah, Mahadura Kama, siman 93) against reciting l’shem yichud, and the story of the change of poshim to chassidim in the closing pasuk of Hoshea, see the discussion in Igros Kodesh of the Rebbe and in Alfasi, HaChassidus MiDor l’Dor.
13. רַב צָדוֹק הַכֹּהֵן מִלּוּבְּלִין, תַּקָּנַת הַשָּׁבִים. Rav Tzadok HaKohen Rabinowitz of Lublin (1823–1900). The Rav retold the biographical background: Rav Tzadok’s trajectory from his early mitnaggedish setting through his encounters with the Chiddushei HaRim of Ger (who was the one rav to refuse to sign for the divorce he sought), through Ishbitz and Lublin. In Takanas HaShavim he acknowledges the Ba’al Shem Tov’s reading (via Rav Yeiva Sar) and the Kli Yakar in Chukas, and still concludes that the literal reenactment cannot be right — the sole exception being the Yerushalmi’s picture of David HaMelech testing himself with his pilagshim, where the fallback is a permitted bi’ah.
14. The מִיטֶעלֶער רֶבִּי’ס dream — recorded by the Frierdiker Rebbe; explicated by the Rebbe in several sichos. The core reference is Sefer HaSichos of the Rayatz (1943 and following); the Rebbe returns to the story in the ma’amarim and sichos of Yud-Tes Kislev and Elul. On the underlying question — who is more secure, the tzaddik or the ba’al teshuvah? — the Rebbe’s footnote resolving the apparent contradiction with the Maggid’s vort on Rashi (Vayikra 20:26) via the split between teshuvah me’yirah and teshuvah me’ahavah is in Likkutei Sichos, chelek 17, Kedoshim.
15. בְּרָכוֹת לד ע"ב — the debate of Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Abahu; the reading of the רַמָ"ע מִפָּאנוֹ and the שְׁלָ"ה הַקָּדוֹשׁ. The Rambam pesken’s like Rabbi Abahu (Hilchos Teshuvah 7:4) and amplifies the gemara’s language: not merely ein omdim but ein yecholim la’amod. The Maharsha rereads the whole gemara as concerning two people who have never sinned. The Ramo MiPano (Sefer Asarah Ma’amaros) and the Shlah HaKadosh (Sha’ar HaOsios) hold that Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Abahu are speaking of two different kinds of penitent — me’yirah vs. me’ahavah — and are not, in truth, arguing at all. The Rebbe cites this at length in Igros Kodesh. This is the resolution that allows the Ba’al Shem Tov’s ma’amar to stand against the Noda BiYehuda’s kal vachomer: it survives because the subject of the entire literal-reenactment tradition is precisely the ba’al teshuvah me’ahavah.
