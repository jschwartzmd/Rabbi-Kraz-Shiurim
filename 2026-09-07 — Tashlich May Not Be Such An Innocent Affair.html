תַּשְׁלִיךְ — מִנְהָג תָּמִים אוֹ פִּרְצָה עַל שְׂפַת הַנָּהָר
Tashlich May Not Be Such An Innocent Affair
A Shiur by Rabbi Yisroel Kraz · Erev Rosh HaShana 5787
Recorded: Elul 5786 · Prepared: 26 Elul 5786 / September 8, 2026

Walking to the water on the afternoon of the first day of Rosh HaShana, shaking the corners of one's garments, reciting Mi keil kamocha and the thirteen middos of rachamim — the minhag of Tashlich has come to feel as fixed a part of the day as the shofar itself. And yet, when one traces its sources, Tashlich turns out to be one of the most contested minhagim on the Jewish calendar. Great poskim — the Maharil, the Ari, the Rema, the Chida — embraced it. Others — among them the Vilna Gaon in his personal conduct, and a chorus of acharonim — either refrained from it, restricted it, or wanted it abolished outright. Behind the picturesque scene at the river lies a serious dispute about superstition, about the mingling of men and women, and about whether a symbolic gesture can ever be honestly mistaken for teshuvah.

I. The Custom That Almost Wasn't

The classical minhag is described in the Rema (Orach Chaim 583:2): after Mincha on the first day of Rosh HaShana, before sunset, one walks to a river, ocean, or well — ideally outside the city — that contains living fish, and there recites the pesukim of Mi keil kamocha from the end of Michah, culminating in the phrase וְתַשְׁלִיךְ בִּמְצֻלוֹת יָם כָּל חַטֹּאתָם, "and You shall cast into the depths of the sea all their sins."16 From this last phrase the custom draws its name.

Simple enough — and yet almost every phrase of that description has been argued about. When to go (the first day, the second day, the fourth day of the Aseres Yemei Teshuva?). Where to go (a river with fish? any body of water? a well?). Who should go (men only? men and women together? children?). What to say. And most fundamentally — whether to go at all. The Maharil, the Bach, and the Shelah stood firmly for the minhag; but a striking undercurrent of poskim — culminating in the Chasam Sofer's remarks and in the Vilna Gaon's personal conduct — treated it as at best a chumra and at worst a stumbling block.

II. Origins: The Maharil, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Midrash

The earliest full description of Tashlich is in the Maharil (Rabbi Yaakov Moellin of Mainz, d. 1427), whose Minhagim record that on the first day of Rosh HaShana, after the seudah, one goes to a river or a well and recites certain pesukim — and, if he has forgotten on the first day, he does so on the second.1 The Maharil grounds the practice in a midrash: when Avraham Avinu went to the Akeidah, the Satan placed a great river before him to block his path; Avraham entered the river up to his neck — כִּי בָאוּ מַיִם עַד נָפֶשׁ — and continued on his mission.2 Going to water on Rosh HaShana, then, is a re-enactment of Avraham's fidelity under trial: a declaration that we, too, will cross whatever water the Satan places in our way.

כָּתַב מהרי"ל שֶׁנּוֹהֲגִין לֵילֵךְ אֶל הַנָּהָר וְלוֹמַר תָּשׁוּב יְרַחֲמֵנוּ, מִשּׁוּם דְּאִיתָא בַּמִּדְרָשׁ (יַלְקוּט וַיֵּרָא צ"ט) שֶׁכְּשֶׁהָלַךְ אַבְרָהָם אָבִינוּ לַעֲקֵדָה שָׂם הַשָּׂטָן נָהָר עַד צַוָּאר, וְאָמַר "הוֹשִׁיעָה ה' כִּי בָאוּ מַיִם עַד נָפֶשׁ". — Bais Yosef quoting the Maharil, on the basis of Yalkut Shimoni, Vayera 99  2

Rabbeinu Yerucham already knows the practice, and adds the detail that one goes to a body of water with living fish, since fish (whose eyes are always open) are a symbol of the ever-open Eye of Providence — עַיִן פְּקוּחָה — and of the tzibbur that flourishes without ayin hara.17

III. The Kabbalistic Layer — The Ari and the Zohar

The Ari"zal took the minhag with tremendous seriousness. The Pri Etz Chaim and Sha'ar HaKavanos preserve his practice: he would go to the water at the moment of shki'ah, contemplating specific yichudim, and the shaking-out of the tzitzis (נִעֲרוּ שׁוּלֵי הַבְּגָדִים) was understood as the literal dislodging of nourishment (yenikah) from the kelipos.8 The Ari also introduced the detail that one ought to look into the water — not at the fish, but at one's own reflection — to bring the tikkun home.

The Zohar (Emor, and again in Parshas Nasso) hints at a related idea: on Rosh HaShana the din is aroused above, and the mekubalim explain that the sea is the domain of Malchus, which absorbs and neutralizes the harshness of the day.22 Casting sins into the depths means offering them up to a place where the accusing forces cannot retrieve them.

וּמִנְהָג שֶׁכָּל יִשְׂרָאֵל תּוֹרָה הוּא, בַּמֶּה שֶׁהוֹלְכִים עַל מַיִם וְאוֹמְרִים "תַּשְׁלִיךְ" בַּמְצוּלוֹת יָם כָּל חַטֹּאתֵינוּ, לֶהֱוֵי, כִּי מִן מְצוּלוֹת יָם שֶׁהוֹלְכִים עַל מַיִם וְאוֹמְרִים "תַּשְׁלִיךְ" הוּא הַתְּהוֹם, וְהִיא הַמָּקוֹם הַיּוֹתֵר עָמוֹק בַּיָּם, לְפִי הַטֶּבַע שֶׁל הַיְסוֹד שֶׁהַמַּיִם יִכַּסּוּ הָאָרֶץ, וְהָאָרֶץ הִיא הַמֶּרְכָּז, וְהוּא מָקוֹם הֶעֱלֵם. — Elyah Rabbah / Shelah, drush on Tashlich, developing the Ari  13

IV. Why Fish, Why Water

The Rema records three overlapping reasons for going specifically to living fish:15,16

First, fish have no eyelids — their eyes are always open. They evoke the Divine ayin pekuchah that is asked to watch over the community, and the reminder that just as fish can be caught in a net without warning, so, too, we can be caught in the net of din without warning — and Rosh HaShana is precisely the day to remember it.17

Second, ayin hara has no power over fish that are hidden in water. We ask that our community be like the fish — וְנִפְרֶה וְנִרְבֶּה כַּדָּגִים — multiplying beneath a surface that jealous eyes cannot penetrate.16

Third — and this is the Ari's emphasis — the sea itself represents the reservoir into which the sins are cast. It is not that the water "washes" us; it is that the ocean is the metaphysical repository into which iniquity is dispatched.

וְנוֹהֲגִים לֵילֵךְ אֶל הַנָּהָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ דָּגִים חַיִּים לְסִימָן שֶׁלֹּא תִּשְׁלוֹט בָּנוּ עַיִן הָרַע וְנִפְרֶה וְנִרְבֶּה כַּדָּגִים, וְטוֹב שֶׁיִּהְיֶה מִחוּץ לָעִיר, וְיֵשׁ לֵילֵךְ בְּיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן שֶׁל רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה אַחַר מִנְחָה קוֹדֶם שְׁקִיעַת הַחַמָּה, וְלוֹמַר פָּסוּק "מִי אֵל כָּמוֹךָ" וְגוֹ'. — Rema, Orach Chaim 583:2  16

V. The Ruzhiner and the Delayed Tashlich

An unusual practice preserved in the sefer Ohel Yesharim (and echoed in several chassidic sources) records that the Ruzhiner Rebbe would not go to Tashlich on the first day of Rosh HaShana at all — not even on the second — but rather on the fourth day of the Aseres Yemei Teshuva (yom dalet d'aseres yemei teshuva), corresponding to Wednesday.5

הָרוּזִ'ינֶר הָיָה הוֹלֵךְ לְתַשְׁלִיךְ לֹא בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה כִּי אִם בְּיוֹם י"ג מִדּוֹת (יוֹם ד' דַּעֲשֶׂרֶת יְמֵי תְשׁוּבָה). [וְהוֹסִיף כ"ק מוּ"ח אַדְמוֹ"ר שְׁלִיטָ"א: בַּהְיוֹתִי בְּאַרְצוֹת הַבְּרִית — נִיוּ יָארְק — אֶשְׁתָּקַד, הָלַכְתִּי לְתַשְׁלִיךְ יָמִים אֲחָדִים לִפְנֵי רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, בְּמוֹצָאֵי רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה וְלִי"ג מִדּוֹתָיו.] — Ohel Yesharim / Chassidishe minhag  5

The theory behind the delay: on the first day, one is preoccupied with the awesome tefillos of the day itself; Tashlich, which is essentially a plea for the thirteen middos of rachamim from Michah, is more fittingly delayed to the day of yud gimmel middos. And when travel makes it necessary — as the annotation from the Rebbe records for his own visit to New York — one may push it even earlier, into the days before Rosh HaShana.

VI. The Opposition — Symbol vs. Substance

Several acharonim were sharply uncomfortable with Tashlich, and their reasons converge on one theme: the danger that a symbolic gesture displaces the real work of teshuvah. A striking passage cited in this vein warns:

דֵּעוֹת נִמְצָאִים אֵיזוֹ אֲשֶׁר סוֹבְרִים וְחוֹשְׁבִים שֶׁאֵין צָרִיךְ שׁוּם הִשְׁתַּדְּלוּת וְהִשְׁתָּרְרוּת אוֹ כַּוָּונָה אוֹ הִתְעוֹרְרוּת בַּחֲלִיכָתָם וְהִלּוּכָם עַל כָּל אֵחָד אַשְׁרֵי לוֹ. עַצְמוֹ, מֵחֲמַת שֶׁאֵין בְּיָדוֹ רַק לִזְרוֹק עַצְמוֹ אֶל הַנָּהָר וּלְהַפְקִיר אֶת עַצְמוֹ מִתּוֹךְ אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה — וְאֶפְשָׁר שֶׁכְּבָר עָלָה בְּמַחֲשַׁבְתּוֹ הַשָּׁטָן לְהַסִּיתוֹ… — Anonymous drush appended to a Rosh HaShana kuntres, warning against Tashlich-as-alibi  6

The critique is not that the water is powerless, but that a Yid who thinks the water does the work has fundamentally misread the pasuk. The Rambam, in Hilchos Teshuva, sets the register: דַּרְכֵי הַתְּשׁוּבָה are made of concrete ma'asim — regret, confession, resolve, and restitution — not of gestures. Chazal did not include a Tashlich-style ritual in the maseches of Rosh HaShana or in the halachos of teshuvah for exactly this reason.7

Later authorities extend the critique in stronger halachic language. The Vilna Gaon, in Ma'aseh Rav and in his conduct, is recorded as not having gone to Tashlich (רָם לֹא הָיָה הוֹלֵךְ לְנָדֵר אוֹ לְבַאֵר לוֹמַר תַּשְׁלִיךְ).18 And a long passage cited by later poskim describes an entire school of gedolim who wished the minhag abolished:

כַּמָּה פְּעָמִים נֶחְרְמוּ, וְעַ"כ בְּתַשְׁלִיךְ הָוֵי מִנְהָג נֶחֱמָס לְגַמְרֵי לַנְמְרֵי מֵחֲמַת שֶׁוַּדַאי אֵין רָאוּי לִנְהוֹג כְּלָל בָּזֶה, וְגַם מַעִיקָרָא לֹא נִיתַּן הַסַּנְהֶדְּרִין כ"א לִגְדוֹלֵי חִקְרֵי לֵב לַחְקוֹר אַחֲרָיו בְּכָל דִּבְרוֹתָם בְּצִנְעָא, אֲבָל בְּזוֹהַ"ק פֵּרוּשׁ מְרוּבָּה עַל הָעוֹמֵד מֵאָה פְּעָמִים, וְכַמָּה קִלְקוּלִים גְּדוֹלִים רָאִיתִי בָּזֶה וְלֹא"ל. וּזְבָעִיקָר כִּי כָּל הַמְּקוֹמוֹת אֲשֶׁר אֵצֶל הַנָּהָר אֵינָם נְקִיִּים בְּכָל צַד, וְלֹא יַסְלִים לְהִיוֹת דָּבָר מְאוֹם בְּתוֹךְ ד' אַמּוֹת, אוֹ שֶׁלֹּא עֵינָיו… וְנָם מָהֳרַבָּנִים הַגְּדוֹלִים אֲשֶׁר קָמוּ עָבָה מִסְפָּתָנוּ, בִּקַּשְׁתִּי מְאֹד שֶׁיֵּעָשׂוּ אִיסוּר זֶה בְּצֵרוּף הַסָּנִיף שֶׁל אִיסוּר עַל בַּעֲיקָר אֲנִי נָדַר בְּאִיסוּר גָּדוֹל וּגְזֵרַת הַתּוֹרָה, שֶׁהַנָּשִׁים לֹא יֵלְכוּ בַּכְּלָל לַעֲשׂוֹת מִנְהָג זֶה. — Extended drush against the current practice of Tashlich  21

Two problems are folded together in the passage: first, the content of Tashlich risks becoming an alibi for teshuvah; second, the social reality of Tashlich — where women and men come together at the river — is a michshol. Both objections come from within the tradition, not from outside it.

VII. The Halachic Concern of Mixed Crowds

The most consistent and practical concern in the acharonim is the mixed-crowd problem. The Chasam Sofer (Orach Chaim 583) and others record it in halachic language: when the practical outcome of a minhag is a chevrasa of women and men gathered on the riverbank, one must ask whether the minhag as observed is still the minhag as instituted. The Bach was already troubled; the Munkatcher, the Chasam Sofer, and later the Steipler recorded that in such places it is better that men not go at all.19

הַשְׁנָהֲגוּ לֵילֵךְ אֶל הַנָּהָר וְלוֹמַר פְּסוּקִים "וְתַשְׁלִיךְ בִּמְצוּלוֹת יָם כָּל חַטֹּאתֵינוּ", וְקוֹרִין לָזֶה תַּשְׁלִיךְ. וְיִזָּהֲרוּ שֶׁלֹּא יֵלְכוּ נָשִׁים, וּבִמְקוֹמוֹת הַרְבֵּה מוּטָב שֶׁאֲנָשִׁים לֹא יֵלְכוּ כְּלָל, וְרַבִּים נִמְנָעִים מִזֶּה מִפְּנֵי טַעַם הַיָּדוּעַ לָהֶם, וְכֵן נָכוֹן הוּא. — Cited as summary of the Bach and later acharonim  19

The Rema himself qualifies the matter in Orach Chaim 583:3, where he adds that katanim v'nashim are not obligated to walk to the river, and the neighborhood-level halachic literature (from the Chida onward) tends to be strict about their attendance in mixed venues.20

VIII. Coda — What Tashlich Is, and What It Isn't

Set against this full history, the picturesque scene at the river takes on a very different weight. The Maharil, Rabbeinu Yerucham, the Ari, the Rema, and the Shelah all embraced Tashlich, understanding it as a piece of the day's tikkun. The Rambam did not know it as halacha. The Vilna Gaon did not go. The Chasam Sofer wanted men in many places not to go. The Ruzhiner delayed it until the fourth day. The reformers-from-within wanted it abolished on the grounds that the crowds had become a michshol and the symbolism had become a substitute for teshuvah.

The synthesis that emerges is the one the sefer implicitly proposes: Tashlich is legitimate as a shell that contains real hishtadlus. Walked slowly, said quietly, contemplated with the Ari's kavanos or with the Maharil's midrash, it is a beautiful act. Walked to as a family outing that displaces the day's own teshuvah, or to a bank where the mixed crowd itself is the ma'aseh, it becomes precisely the minhag that the acharonim — each in his own generation — tried to reclaim from the water's edge.

The absence of Tashlich in the Rambam has been read by more than one acharon as an active reservation.
8. הָאֲרִיזַ"ל — פְּרִי עֵץ חַיִּים / שַׁעַר הַכַּוָּונוֹת, drush on Tashlich. "כָּתַב שׁוּטָנוּ בּוֹ וּמַסְטִינֵנוּ הַגְּלוּיִים מִלַּמּוּ שֶׁפַּעֲמוֹן חוֹתָם אָפְנֵי חוֹגָל…" The Ari's kavanos at Tashlich focus on the shaking-out of the tzitzis as the literal dislodging of yenikas ha'kelipos, and on gazing into the water as a means of restoring Malchus to her proper place. See Pri Etz Chaim, Sha'ar Rosh HaShana, perek 5.
9. רַבֵּינוּ יְרוּחָם, sefer Toldos Adam v'Chava, nesiv 5. Rabbeinu Yerucham (14th c., Provence) already knows the minhag and roots it in the fish-as-open-eye of Providence. His formulation is the source for the Rema's requirement that the water contain living fish.
10. נָכוֹן לֵילֵךְ בְּיוֹם א' דר"ה קוֹדֶם שְׁקִיעַת הַחַמָּה. Summary formulation preserved in later poskim: proper time is the first day of Rosh HaShana, before shki'ah; proper place is a spring or well of mayim chayim, ideally outside the city; proper text includes the yud gimmel middos. See Mateh Ephraim 598:4 and Elef HaMagen ad loc.
11. וְכֵן מַה שֶּׁנֶּהְגִין לוֹמַר הַפָּסוּק "תַּשְׁלִיךְ בִּמְצֻלוֹת יָם חַטֹּאתֵינוּ". A drush explaining the pasuk as it functions in the minhag: the "depths of the sea" are the metaphysical repository into which the sins are dispatched; the very act of the tzibbur reciting the phrase constitutes the tashlich, not the water. Compare Levush 583 and Elyah Rabbah ad loc.
12. וְהוֹלְכִין אֶל [ז'] הַנָּהָר וְלוֹמַר פָּסוּק "וְתַשְׁלִיךְ בִּמְצוּלוֹת יָם כָּל חַטֹּאתֵינוּ". Continuation of the previous drush, adjusting the language for various nusachos. The bracketed zayin (a printer's correction) reflects the well-known variant reading in the older machzorim.
13. שֶׁלֹּ"ה / אֶלְיָה רַבָּה, drush on Tashlich as a Zohar-based practice. "וּמִנְהָג שֶׁכָּל יִשְׂרָאֵל תּוֹרָה הוּא, בַּמֶּה שֶׁהוֹלְכִים עַל מַיִם וְאוֹמְרִים תַּשְׁלִיךְ…" The Shelah develops the Ari's teaching that the sea corresponds to Malchus, and that ta'shlich bimtzulos yam is metaphysically the dispatching of sin to the domain where accusation cannot retrieve it.
14. וְעוֹד יֵשׁ טַעַם, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁאָנוּ מַמְלִיכִים הַיּוֹם אֶת הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא. "שֶׁאָנוּ מַמְלִיכִים הַיּוֹם אֶת הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא — בָּרוּךְ עָלֵינוּ, וְהַדֶּרֶךְ הוּא לְשַׂם אֶת הַמְּלָכִים אֵצֶל הַנָּהָר, לְרֶמֶז שֶׁתִּמָּשֵׁךְ מַלְכוּתְךָ." A second reason: since Rosh HaShana is the day of coronating the King, and coronations were traditionally performed at a river (as with Shlomo HaMelech at the Gichon), we go to the river as a re-enactment of the coronation.
15. וּרְגִילִין לֵילֵךְ לְמָקוֹם שֶׁרוֹאִין שָׁם ג' דָּגִים. A specific detail: some are careful to go to a place where three fish can be seen, as a siman that the tzibbur will not be caught in the net of the yetzer, in the net of the enemy, or in the net of the malach ha'maves. See Mateh Ephraim 598:7.
16. רְמָ"א, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקפ"ג סָעִיף ב. "וְנוֹהֲגִים לֵילֵךְ אֶל הַנָּהָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ דָּגִים חַיִּים לְסִימָן שֶׁלֹּא תִּשְׁלוֹט בָּנוּ עַיִן הָרַע וְנִפְרֶה וְנִרְבֶּה כַּדָּגִים… וְטוֹב שֶׁיִּהְיֶה מִחוּץ לָעִיר וְיֵשׁ לֵילֵךְ בְּיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן שֶׁל רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה אַחַר מִנְחָה קוֹדֶם שְׁקִיעַת הַחַמָּה, וְלוֹמַר פָּסוּק 'מִי אֵל כָּמוֹךָ' וְגוֹ'." This is the classical codification. Sefaria ref: Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 583:2 (Rema's gloss).
17. Marginal note on fish as siman. "אַחַר מִנְחָה יוֹם א' דְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה קוֹדֶם שְׁקִיעַת הַחַמָּה טוֹב לֵילֵךְ מִחוּץ לָעִיר אֶל הַנַּחַל אוֹ מַעְיָן כִּי מַיִם מוֹרִים עַל הַחֶסֶד, וְגַם דָּגִים רוֹמְזִים עַל עַיִן פְּקוּחָה." A gloss on the double symbolism of water (chesed) and fish (open eye of Providence). Rooted in Bereishis Rabbah 97:3.
18. רָם לֹא הָיָה הוֹלֵךְ לְנָהָר אוֹ לְבַאֵר לוֹמַר תַּשְׁלִיךְ — Ma'aseh Rav / Vilna Gaon. The GRA's personal conduct, as recorded in Ma'aseh Rav (and referenced in Nishmas Adam), was not to go to Tashlich. Combined with his general suspicion of gestures uncodified by the Rambam or the Bavli, this is the strongest single testimony against the practice within the mainstream tradition.
19. Summation preserved in the acharonim: mixed crowds. "וְיִזָּהֲרוּ שֶׁלֹּא יֵלְכוּ נָשִׁים, וּבִמְקוֹמוֹת הַרְבֵּה מוּטָב שֶׁאֲנָשִׁים לֹא יֵלְכוּ כְּלָל, וְרַבִּים נִמְנָעִים מִזֶּה מִפְּנֵי טַעַם הַיָּדוּעַ לָהֶם." The ta'am ha'yadu'a lahem is the mixed-crowd concern. The Chasam Sofer (OC 583) and the Steipler both endorsed the position that in such places men should not go at all.
20. רָמָ"א, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקצ"ג ס"ק י' — on children and women. "וְיֵ"ל דִּלְקַטְנִים וְנָשִׁים אֵין חִיּוּב לֵילֵךְ אֶל הַנָּהָר." The Rema himself, in the parallel siman, records that katanim v'nashim are not obligated to walk to the river. Later acharonim (including the Chida) extend this into a positive discouragement in mixed venues.
21. וְכֵן בְּתַשְׁלִיךְ ק"ו בֶּן ב"ב שֶׁל ק"ו — extended drush against current practice. "כִּי רָאוּי לְבַטֵּל הַמִּנְהָג שֶׁל תַּשְׁלִיךְ לְגַמְרֵי מַחְמַת… כַּמָּה קִלְקוּלִים גְּדוֹלִים רָאִיתִי בָּזֶה…" A remarkably strong passage — likely from the Chasam Sofer's school — in which the author calls for outright abolition of the practice as it was being observed, listing three failures: proximity of tumah, mixed crowds, and the reduction of teshuvah to a gesture. The author asks the great rabbanim of his generation to join in a formal cherem against women's attendance.
22. הֲלִיכַת תַּשְׁלִיךְ — זוה"ק פָּרָשַׁת אֱמוֹר. "וּמִנְהָג זֶה יֵשׁ לוֹ מָקוֹר גַּם בַּזּוֹהַ"ק (פ' אֱמוֹר) וְזַ"ל." The Zohar-based source: the sea corresponds to Malchus, and casting into it is the dispatching of din. This is the mekubalim's answer to the objection that Chazal did not codify the minhag — it is codified in the Zohar, which has halachic weight in matters of minhag. Sefaria ref: Zohar, Emor, section on Rosh HaShana.
