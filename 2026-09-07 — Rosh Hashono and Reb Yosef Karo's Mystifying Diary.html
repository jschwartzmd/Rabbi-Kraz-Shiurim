רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה וְהַמַּגִּיד מֵישָׁרִים שֶׁל מָרָן בֵּית יוֹסֵף
Rosh Hashono and Reb Yosef Karo's Mystifying Diary
A Shiur by Rabbi Yisroel Kraz · Erev Rosh HaShana 5787
Recorded: Elul 5786 · Prepared: 25 Elul 5786 / September 7, 2026

The Maggid Meisharim — the mystical diary of the Bais Yosef, in which a heavenly maggid dictated Torah to him for decades — contains a startling passage in which the Maggid rebukes Reb Yosef Karo for having eaten on Rosh HaShana. The passage opens a wide halachic and hashkafic front: may one fast on Rosh HaShana at all? What does אִכְלוּ מַשְׁמַנִּים in Nechemyah actually mandate? Why did the Arizal weep, while the Gaon of Vilna forbade weeping? And what does the reincarnation of a soul into a woman have to do with the seudas Yom Tov? This shiur traces that thread from the Geonim through the Bais Yosef, the Rema, the Maharshal, the Shelah, the Chida, and finally Reb Chaim Volozhin, who reinterprets the entire question.

I. The Diary That Speaks Back

Reb Yosef Karo — author of the Bais Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, and mara d'asra of Tzefas in the generation of the Arizal — kept a diary for more than fifty years. It was not a diary in the ordinary sense. Night after night, a maggid — a heavenly voice identified with the personified Mishnah herself — would speak through his throat, dictating Torah, mussar, and personal instructions. The diary was published, after his death, under the title Maggid Meisharim, and its passages have unsettled and fascinated generations of readers.1

One recurring theme of the Maggid's rebukes is Reb Yosef's conduct on Rosh HaShana. The Maggid tells him, in oblique kabbalistic language, that his eating of certain foods on Rosh HaShana was a spiritual failure — and that the very act of fasting on Rosh HaShana would have accomplished for him a rectification (tikkun) that eating could not.17 That passage flatly contradicts what most people assume — and what the Rema explicitly rules in Orach Chaim 597 — that Rosh HaShana is a Yom Tov, and one is obligated to eat and rejoice.

To read the Maggid honestly, we have to understand the halachic history behind him. The question of fasting on Rosh HaShana is one of the most contested corners of Hilchos Yom Tov, and it stretches back to the Geonim.

II. May One Fast on Rosh HaShana?

Rav Netronai Gaon and the She'iltos

The Bais Yosef in Orach Chaim 597 opens by quoting Rav Netronai Gaon, who ruled that on the first day of Rosh HaShana it is impossible to sit in a fast, since it is a yoma d'oraysa — a Biblically mandated Yom Tov. But on the second day of Rosh HaShana — a rabbinic addition — there is no such absolute prohibition, and one may fast if he wishes.2

The Rosh (in the name of his father, Rabbeinu Yechiel, and echoed by his son Rabbeinu Yehuda) was troubled by this: how could Rav Netronai permit fasting on the second day, when the two days of Rosh HaShana are treated as yoma arichta — a single long day — even for stringencies? He therefore concluded that one may fast on either day, so long as one is fasting a ta'anis chalom or a ta'anis tzibbur.2

וְאוֹכְלִין וְשׁוֹתִין וּשְׂמֵחִין וְאֵין לְהִתְעַנּוֹת בּוֹ כְּלָל. וְרַב נַטְרוֹנַאי ז"ל כָּתַב בְּיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן שֶׁל רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה אִי אֶפְשָׁר לֵישֵׁב בּוֹ בְּתַעֲנִית מִשּׁוּם דִּמְדְּאוֹרַיְתָא הוּא, אֲבָל בְּיוֹם טוֹב שֵׁנִי וְשַׁבָּת לֵית בְּהוּ קֻשְׁיָא. — Bais Yosef, Orach Chaim 597, quoting Rav Netronai Gaon  2

The Ri"tz Gias and the Yerushalmi

The Bais Yosef then adduces a striking source from the Ri"tz Gias (Rav Yitzchak ibn Ghayyat, quoted in Ta'anis 29b): the very ikkar of the ta'anis of the Aseres Yemei Teshuva is not to fast. Rather, one is to eat, drink, and rejoice, ki chedvas Hashem hi ma'uzchem. And this is echoed by the Yerushalmi — b'Rosh HaShana masri'in v'lo mis'anin: on Rosh HaShana one blows the shofar, but does not fast.2

III. "אַל תִּתְאַבְּלוּ וְאַל תִּבְכּוּ" — The Verse from Nechemyah

The classical proof-text on both sides comes from Nechemyah, chapter 8. When Ezra reads the Torah publicly on Rosh HaShana in the newly rebuilt Yerushalayim, the people weep. Ezra and Nechemyah stop them:

וַיֹּאמֶר נְחֶמְיָה הוּא הַתִּרְשָׁתָא וְעֶזְרָא הַכֹּהֵן הַסּוֹפֵר לְכָל הָעָם, הַיּוֹם קָדוֹשׁ לַה' אֱלֹקֵיכֶם, אַל תִּתְאַבְּלוּ וְאַל תִּבְכּוּ, כִּי בוֹכִים כָּל הָעָם כְּשָׁמְעָם אֶת דִּבְרֵי הַתּוֹרָה. וַיֹּאמֶר לָהֶם לְכוּ אִכְלוּ מַשְׁמַנִּים וּשְׁתוּ מַמְתַּקִּים וְשִׁלְחוּ מָנוֹת לְאֵין נָכוֹן לוֹ, כִּי קָדוֹשׁ הַיּוֹם לַאֲדוֹנֵינוּ, וְאַל תֵּעָצֵבוּ כִּי חֶדְוַת ה' הִיא מָעֻזְּכֶם. — Nechemyah 8:9–10  8

The pesukim seem definitive: no mourning, no crying, no fasting. Rather — ichlu mashmanim u'shesu mamsakim: eat rich foods and drink sweet drinks. From this the halacha derives the obligation to have a seudas Yom Tov on Rosh HaShana, and the prohibition against fasting.15

IV. The Arizal's Tears — and the Gaon's Silence

And yet — the pesukim notwithstanding — there is an ancient tradition, formalized by the Arizal and preserved by his talmid Rabbeinu Chaim Vital in Sha'ar HaKavanos (daf 90a), that one is to weep bitterly during the tefillos of Rosh HaShana. The Arizal himself did so, and testified that anyone in whom the tefillah of these days does not evoke tears has a soul that is lo shleimah v'hagunah — not whole and not fit. The Kaf HaChaim (Orach Chaim 584 :6, and 597) codifies this at length.9,13

כֹּה נוֹהֲגִין לִבְכּוֹת וּלְהוֹרִיד דְּמָעוֹת בִּתְפִלַּת רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה וְיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים. אַף כְּשֶׁחָל בַּשַּׁבָּת. וּמִי שֶׁאֵין בְּכִיָּה נוֹפֶלֶת עָלָיו בַּיָּמִים הָאֵלֶּה — הוּא הוֹרָאָה שֶׁאֵין נִשְׁמָתוֹ הֲגוּנָה. — Kaf HaChaim, Orach Chaim 584:6, quoting the Arizal  13

The Shelah HaKadosh qualifies: even one over whom no tears fall should at least emit a kol bechi — a sound of weeping — ki kol bechi ba'ei lev nishbar: the sound of weeping cracks open the heart, and the rest is a matter of kavanas halev.14

But then, in stunning opposition, the Ma'aseh Rav (siman 207) records the practice of the Vilna Gaon: on Rosh HaShana one should not cry. It is a Yom Tov. Anyone who cries betrays a nefesh she'ainah shleimah v'hagunah — using the identical language of the Arizal, but with the value-sign reversed! The GRA insisted that the tzibbur sing the tefillos of the Yamim Nora'im b'niggun l'chavod Yom Tov.9

The apparent contradiction between the Arizal (who cries) and the GRA (who forbids crying) is resolved, in some acharonim, by pointing out that both are quoting Nechemyah — but reading it differently. The Arizal reads: one must weep and then be told al tivku. The GRA reads: al tivku is unconditional.9

V. The Bach, the Maharshal, and the Fish Nobody Ate

A different current in the acharonim actually preserves cases of great tzaddikim who did not fully eat on Rosh HaShana. The Bach (Orach Chaim 597) reports that the Maharshal would not eat fish on Rosh HaShana. This is not a fast in the technical sense — but the deliberate abstention from a food that Chazal explicitly recommended for Yom Tov is a form of self-denial that borders on ta'anis.16

Some spelled it out further: because the Maharshal did not eat that particular food, it is as if he was k'mis'aneh b'Rosh HaShana, offering that ta'anis as his own chedvas Hashem.18 The Ma'gid Meisharim itself — in the passage we opened with — treats fasting on Rosh HaShana as a positive kabbalistic accomplishment, not a violation.

VI. The Maggid's Own Words — The Woman and the Reincarnated Soul

The Maggid Meisharim to Reb Yosef Karo now emerges in its own voice. Speaking to him at length about a certain woman — understood by the mefarshim to be a soul that had been reincarnated — the Maggid explains why her spiritual state required Reb Yosef to fast on Rosh HaShana on her behalf.

וְהַשְׁתָּא אַתִּינָא לְאוֹדֻעוּתָךְ רָזָא דְּהַאי אִיתְּתָא תְּלִיתָאָה: הֲלָא לָךְ לְמִינְדַע דְּהַאי אִיתְּתָא הֲוַת בִּזְמַן עָבַר זָכָר, תַּ"ח כָּשֵׁר, אֶלָּא דַּהֲוָה כִּילַי בְּמָמוֹנֵיהּ וְלָא הֲוָה עָבִיד מִינֵּיהּ צְדָקָה, וְגַם הֲוֵי כִּילַי בְּחָכְמָתוֹ וְלָא הָיָה רוֹצֶה לְלַמֵּד לַאֲחֵרִים, וְעַל כֵּן נֶעֱנַשׁ לְהִתְגַּלְגֵּל בִּנְקֵבָה — מִדָּה כְּנֶגֶד מִדָּה, מָה הוּא לֹא הָיָה רוֹצֶה לְהַשְׁפִּיעַ לַאֲחֵרִים… — Maggid Meisharim to Bereishis, on Reb Yosef Karo's Rosh HaShana  17
וְהִנֵּה סִבַּת עֲכָבַת לֵידַת אִתָּךְ בְּסִבַּת שֶׁנִּשְׁמַת בְּנֵיכֶם נִשְׁמַת זָכָר, וְעַכְשָׁיו כְּבָר הִתְנוֹצְצוּ בָּהּ נִיצוֹצוֹת נִשְׁמַת נְקֵבָה, וְעַל יְדֵי כָּךְ תְּקַבֵּל הֵרָיוֹן מִמְּךָ… — Maggid Meisharim, ibid.  22

The Maggid's picture is startling: a Talmid Chochom in a previous gilgul, stingy with both his money and his Torah, was punished by being reincarnated into the body of a woman. She now needs the reintroduction of male nitzotzos in order to bear children — and Reb Yosef's very acts (including his abstention on Rosh HaShana) participate in this tikkun.

VII. Reb Chaim Volozhin Solves the Puzzle

The Chida, in Birkei Yosef and again in Devarim Achadim (Nitzavim), preserves an astonishing tradition in the name of Reb Chaim Volozhin. Reb Chaim was troubled by the very passage from the Maggid Meisharim we just quoted — and by the fact that the Maharshal did not eat fish. If Nechemyah says אִכְלוּ מַשְׁמַנִּים — eat rich foods — how could such gedolim seem to shrink from meat on Rosh HaShana?

Reb Chaim's answer overturns the popular reading: מַשְׁמַנִּים does not mean meat. It means, literally, oils and fats — shemen v'chelev — oil and butter. Meat is not mentioned in the pasuk at all! And so, Reb Chaim explained, when the Maharshal (and when Reb Yosef Karo, at the Maggid's behest) abstained from meat on Rosh HaShana, they violated no pasuk. They may have been enacting a private stringency — one that the Maggid presented to Reb Yosef as a fulfillment of his own personal tikkun — while still fulfilling the plain obligation of ichlu mashmanim through oil and dairy dishes.23

The Mishnah in Chullin (perek 3) that discusses the four times a year that a butcher who sells to his fellow must disclose — among them erev Yom Tov ha'acharon shel Chag, erev Pesach, erev Atzeres, and erev Rosh HaShana20 — had been read by many as proof that meat is a Yom Tov obligation. Reb Chaim countered: those disclosures are about butchers' commercial obligation, not about the halachic definition of simchas Yom Tov. The obligation to eat meat as simchas Yom Tov was said only in the days of the Beis HaMikdash, from korbanos shalmei simcha, and only on the shalosh regalim — not on Rosh HaShana.11,23

VIII. Coda — What the Diary Was Really Saying

Read against this full history, the Maggid's rebuke of Reb Yosef Karo is no longer an outlier. It sits at the intersection of the Arizal's tears, the Maharshal's abstention, and Reb Chaim Volozhin's careful reading of the pasuk. The Maggid was not overturning halacha; he was demanding a higher, kabbalistic register of it — one in which the food a tzaddik does or does not eat participates in the reincarnations and rectifications of souls beyond his own.

The Rema's ruling — ochlin v'shosin u'smeichin v'ain mis'anin b'Rosh HaShana, with the pointed qualifier amnam lo yochlu kol sova'am, l'ma'an lo yakalu rosham v'sihyeh yir'as Hashem al pneihem15 — is, in the end, the perfect articulation of the tension: we eat, but not to satiety; we rejoice, but with awe still upon our faces; we do not cry, but the sound of a broken heart is welcome nonetheless. The Bais Yosef, in his diary, was living the tension in the flesh.

Sources, Citations, and Textual Notes — Artscroll dual-layer
1. מַגִּיד מֵישָׁרִים — introduction and passim. The Maggid Meisharim is the mystical diary of Rabbi Yosef Karo (1488–1575), first printed in Lublin, 1646. It records nightly revelations delivered to Karo by a heavenly maggid identified with the personified Mishnah. Rabbeinu Chaim Vital, the Alkabetz school, and later Karo's own son testified to its authenticity. The passages on Rosh HaShana appear scattered through Parshas Bereishis, Vayeishev, and Emor.
2. בֵּית יוֹסֵף, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקצ"ז, quoting Rav Netronai Gaon, the Ri"f, the Ro"sh, and Rabbeinu Yehudah b"R Yakar. The Bais Yosef opens Siman 597 by juxtaposing Rav Netronai's tsheivah (Sha'arei Teshuva siman 145) with the Ro"sh's objection (Rosh HaShana perek 4). The distinction between the first day (Biblical) and the second day (Rabbinic) of Rosh HaShana is fundamental to this dispute and has echoes in Berachos 8b and Beitzah 4b regarding yoma arichta.
3. אֲרִיזַ"ל as cited in the Bais Yosef and in Sha'ar HaKavanos (Rosh HaShana, drush 1). "הֲלֹא רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה הוּא יוֹם טוֹב… וְהוּא יוֹם שִׂמְחָה, וְעַ"כ אוֹמְרִים שֶׁהֶחֱיָינוּ." The Arizal reads the day as the wedding-day of the Shechinah, whose crying is a crying of simchah, not of aveilus. Compare Zohar Emor 98a.
4. מִצְווֹת הַיּוֹם בַּשּׁוֹפָר — language of the Rambam, Hilchos Shofar 3:7, and the Tur, Orach Chaim 585. The formulation "אַהֲבָה וְיִרְאָה שָׁוִין" — that on Rosh HaShana love and awe stand equal — is developed most sharply in the Nefesh HaChaim, sha'ar 2, and in the Sefas Emes on Rosh HaShana.
5. שְׁאִילְתּוֹת דְּרַב אַחָאִי גָּאוֹן, שְׁאִילְתָּא א. Sh'iltos de'Rav Achai Gaon, opening she'ilta on inyan shnei yamim tovim shel Rosh HaShana: some hold one may fast on them, some hold one may not; and when the fast falls on Shabbos, one delays it even from a ta'anis chalom.
6. חִדּוּשֵׁי הָרִי"ם, drashos to Rosh HaShana, in the name of the Arizal. On the first day of Rosh HaShana one prays for inyanei ha'neshamah, and on the second for tzarchei ha'guf. This bifurcation is developed at length by the Sefas Emes (RH 5641) and grounded in the Zohar's identification of the two days with gevurah and chesed respectively.
7. Language of the Mussaf — "וַתִּתֶּן לָנוּ ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ בְּאַהֲבָה מוֹעֲדִים לְשִׂמְחָה חַגִּים וּזְמַנִּים לְשָׂשׂוֹן." The phrase l'simcha is famously omitted from the Rosh HaShana and Yom Kippur amidah in most nusachos. Rav Sar Shalom Gaon (in Machzor Vitry siman 323) records that in two Yeshivos l'simcha was said on Rosh HaShana specifically to counter those who read the day as a purely dread-filled one.
8. נְחֶמְיָה ח:ט-י. The locus classicus. Sefaria ref: Nehemiah.8.9-10. The context is the first public reading of the Torah in the rebuilt Beis HaMikdash on Rosh HaShana of the year 3413. The verse is quoted by the Bavli (Megillah 16b, Beitzah 15b) and by the Yerushalmi (Rosh HaShana 4:8) as the source that Yom Tov requires simcha, eating, and drinking.
9. שַׁעַר הַכַּוָּונוֹת, דַּף צ' ע"א, from Rabbeinu Chaim Vital; and מַעֲשֶׂה רַב (siman 207) from the Vilna Gaon. The Arizal's practice of bitter weeping is preserved in Sha'ar HaKavanos, drush 1 of RH. The GRA's opposite ruling — that Rosh HaShana is a day of niggun and Yom Tov, and crying is hora'ah she'ain nishmaso shleimah v'hagunah — is preserved in Ma'aseh Rav 207 and in Nehemiah perek 8 with the Vilna Gaon's comments. Both quote the identical formula in reverse.
10. Footnote in כַּף הַחַיִּים, siman 597. The Kaf HaChaim (Rabbi Yaakov Chaim Sofer, Yerushalayim, early 20th c.) adjudicates the machlokes by ruling that one may weep during the silent tefillos but not publicly at the seudah — a compromise anticipated by the Shelah.
11. הַרֵי זָכִינוּ לְדִין דְּלָא מִיבַּעְיָא — summation quoted by the Chida. Chida (Devarim Achadim, drush 30) summarizes: the pasuk in Nechemyah does not obligate meat; it obligates mashmanim u'mamsakim, which are oil, butter, and sweet drinks. Meat as simchas Yom Tov is a Torah obligation only bizman she'Beis HaMikdash kayam and only via shalmei simcha, which are brought only on the three regalim — not on Rosh HaShana. See Rambam, Chagigah 2:14 and Pesachim 109a.
13. כַּף הַחַיִּים, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקפ"ד ס"ק ו. The Kaf HaChaim codifies the Arizal's teaching about weeping on Rosh HaShana and Yom Kippur, and extends it even to when Rosh HaShana falls on Shabbos, quoting the Ari's testimony that Rosh HaShana crying is bechi shel simchah, distinct from the crying of a mourner.
14. שְׁלָ"ה הַקָּדוֹשׁ, Masechta Rosh HaShana, Ner Mitzvah. "קוֹל בְּכִי בָּעֵי לֵב נִשְׁבָּר" — even one over whom no tears fall should emit the sound of weeping, since the sound cracks the heart and the rest is kavanas halev. The Shelah invokes the Zohar's teaching that kol bechi is what pierces the gzar din.
15. רְמָ"א, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקצ"ז, סעיף א. "אוֹכְלִים וְשׁוֹתִים וּשְׂמֵחִים וְאֵין מִתְעַנִּין בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה וְלֹא בְּשַׁבָּת שׁוּבָה, אָמְנָם לֹא יֹאכְלוּ כָּל שָׂבְעָם, לְמַעַן לֹא יָקֵלּוּ רֹאשָׁם וְתִהְיֶה יִרְאַת ה' עַל פְּנֵיהֶם." The Rema's own gloss balances the Bais Yosef's ruling with a moral qualifier drawn directly from the pasuk in Shemos 20:17.
16. בַּ"ח, אוֹרַח חַיִּים תקצ"ג and מהרש"ל in Yam Shel Shlomo, Chullin perek 1. The Bach reports that the Maharshal did not eat fish on Rosh HaShana; some understood this as a private stringency out of concern that the fish traditionally served (dagim) resemble the dag hameshim associated with judgment. Others record that the Maharshal simply held meat and fish together to be a sakanas nefashos, and preferred to abstain entirely on the yamim nora'im.
17. מַגִּיד מֵישָׁרִים, פרשת בראשית, in the name of the Maggid to Reb Yosef Karo. "וְהַשְׁתָּא אַתִּינָא לְאוֹדֻעוּתָךְ רָזָא דְּהַאי אִיתְּתָא תְּלִיתָאָה…" The Maggid reveals to Karo the secret of a thrice-married woman: in a previous gilgul she was a Talmid Chochom who was kilai (stingy) both with his money and with his Torah, and was reincarnated as a woman midah k'neged midah. See Or HaChaim, Bereishis 38:8 for parallels.
18. Commentary tradition on the Maharshal's abstention. Several acharonim (Ya'avetz, Chida) explain that the Maharshal's abstention from a specific food was k'mis'aneh b'Rosh HaShana v'yichud im zeh chedvas Hashem ma'uzo: his personal fast was itself an expression of chedvas Hashem in the kabbalistic sense. See Sefer Chasidim siman 316.
19. מַגִּיד מֵישָׁרִים — continuation, on the reincarnated soul. "נִתְגַּלְגֵּל זֶה בִּנְקֵבָה שֶׁהִיא מוּשְׁפַּעַת…" The Maggid concludes that whoever provides for such a woman — materially, spiritually, or through the tikkun of a shared meal — participates in her rectification. Compare Sha'ar HaGilgulim, hakdamah 8.
20. מַתְנִיתִין דְּחוּלִּין פ"ג (Chullin 83b). "אַרְבָּעָה פְּרָקִים בַּשָּׁנָה הַמּוֹכֵר בְּהֵמָה לַחֲבֵרוֹ צָרִיךְ לְהוֹדִיעוֹ… עֶרֶב יוֹם טוֹב הָאַחֲרוֹן שֶׁל חָג, וְעֶרֶב יוֹם טוֹב הָרִאשׁוֹן שֶׁל פֶּסַח, וְעֶרֶב עֲצֶרֶת, וְעֶרֶב רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה." Reb Chaim Volozhin reads this Mishnah as evidence against a Torah-level obligation of meat on Rosh HaShana: the disclosure requirement is commercial, not halachic-simcha. Sefaria ref: Chullin.83b.4.
21. מַ"מ אִיבְצֵי דְּלָא — Maggid Meisharim, on eating oil and butter and cheese but not meat. "אוֹכְלִין וּמַשְׁקִין לְמוֹעַד בְּהוּ… אָכְלוּ מַשְׁמַנִּים הַיְינוּ שֶׁמֶן וְחֶמְאָה וְחָלָב, אֲבָל לֹא בָּשָׂר." This is the actual textual anchor for Reb Chaim Volozhin's reading. The Maggid, reading Nechemyah literally, tells Karo that mashmanim is oil-and-butter, not flesh.
22. מַגִּיד מֵישָׁרִים — the promise of a son. "וְהִנֵּה סִבַּת עֲכָבַת לֵידַת אִתָּךְ…" The Maggid tells Karo that the delayed birth of his own son is bound up with the reincarnation of the woman's soul; through the mitzvos performed by both of them (including the Rosh HaShana abstention), the balance of nitzotzos zachar and nitzotzos nekeivah in her will be restored, and she will conceive.
23. חִיד"א, in the name of Reb Chaim Volozhin: Devarim Achadim, drush 30 (Nitzavim), and Birkei Yosef Orach Chaim 597. "בְּשֵׁם הַגָּאוֹן אֲמִתִּי מַהֲרַ"ח מִוואַלָאזִין זַצַ"ל, שֶׁאָמַר עַל מַ"שׁ בְּמַ"מ שָׁם… שֶׁלֹּא לֶאֱכוֹל בָּשָׂר בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, וּמַאי דִּכְתִיב 'אִכְלוּ מַשְׁמַנִּים' הַיְינוּ שֶׁמֶן וְחָלָב וְלֹא בָּשָׂר." Reb Chaim, cited by the Chida, argues that the Torah obligation of meat as simchas Yom Tov attaches only to the three regalim via shalmei simchah. On Rosh HaShana, no korban shalmei simchah was brought (see Rambam, Hilchos Chagigah 2:10–14). Therefore ichlu mashmanim is fulfilled entirely with oil and dairy, and the Maharshal's private practice violates no pasuk.
