בָּא אָדָם עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וְחַיָּה
Adam HaRishon and the Animals
A Manuscript from Prague Claims We Got It Wrong
A Shiur by Rabbi Yisroel Kraz · Monday Evening, 24 Tishrei 5787 · Yahrtzeit of the Ri of Dampierre
Recorded: Monday evening, 24 Tishrei 5787 / October 5, 2026 · Prepared: 25 Tishrei 5787 / October 6, 2026

Rashi on Bereishis 2:23 (רש״י בראשית ב:כג), quoting the gemara in Yevamos 63a, tells us that zos hapaam (זֹאת הַפַּעַם) teaches that Adam HaRishon “came upon every beast and creature, and his mind was not at ease until he came upon Chava.” Read at face value, it is a shocking statement — and almost no one wants to read it at face value. The Maharal asks the halachic question: Adam had already been commanded on arayos. The Torah Temimah asks the biological one: such relations would have left the animals barren. The Levush asks the aesthetic and the practical ones. And a fourteenth-century manuscript from Prague, the Sefer HaNitzachon, records how the Christian disputants used this very Rashi as a weapon. Against all of them stands the Taz, who cheerfully answers every question and insists that Rashi means exactly what he says. This shiur walks through the sheet source by source — from the Rashbam’s entirely different reading of zos hapaam, through Maharshal, Vilna Gaon, Akeidah, Toldos Yitzchak, Chizkuni and Maharsha, to the Taz — and ends, as the Rav’s shiurim so often do, with a live argument still on the table.

I. The Setup — Day One, Hour Five

The Rav began with the pesukim on the first page of the sheet (#1 and #2). The Aibershter creates Adam on the first Friday and places him in Gan Eden — Adam was created outside the Garden and then lifted in — and immediately commands him: מִכֹּל עֵץ הַגָּן אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל, you may eat from every tree, but וּמֵעֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע לֹא תֹאכַל מִמֶּנּוּ.1 The Or Sameach (R’ Meir Simcha of Dvinsk, a gaon who passed away shortly before the Holocaust) notes that achol tochal is not mere permission; it is a mitzvah. He ties it to the well-known Yerushalmi that a person will one day be asked whether he partook of everything permitted that was set before him. Food you ate with the right intent becomes part of you — that slice of pizza ends up in your davening and your learning — and food you refused is an opportunity squandered.

Then comes לֹא טוֹב הֱיוֹת הָאָדָם לְבַדּוֹ, אֶעֱשֶׂה לּוֹ עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ. The gemara has two views on how Chava was formed: Adam was created du-partzufin (דּוּ פַּרְצוּפִין) with two faces and was divided, or — the simple reading — he was alone and the Aibershter took a tzela (צֵלָע). Either way, the Aibershter did not hand Adam a mate unprompted. He first sent him to name every behemah and chayah, and Adam saw that each had a mate. וּלְאָדָם לֹא מָצָא עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ — and Adam found none. Rashi explains: he said, “Everyone has a partner and I do not,” and only then did the Aibershter cast the tardeimah upon him.2

Why did the Aibershter want him to ask? The Rav offered a thought: when Adam later blamed Chava — ha’ishah asher nasata imadi — the Aibershter could answer, “You requested her.” Had Chava been given unasked, Adam could have said he never wanted her. Rashi at 2:18 gives a different reason for the whole arrangement, which was the question of an attendee: the Aibershter is alone above; if Adam were alone below, people might say there are two powers (שְׁתֵּי רָשׁוּיוֹת).2 (To the listener who asked how we know the Aibershter is alone: because Hashem Echad (ה׳ אֶחָד) — He told us so — and He then emphasized it.)

A passing vort from the room: the Aibershter takes His sha’ashuim (שַׁעֲשׁוּעִים) in Torah, and the Torah is, so to speak, His toy — and it is hard to play alone. The Rav added the well-known maaseh that the first thing a tzaddik is asked upon arriving Above is to deliver a shiur, and that the Bach, who had permitted new grain outside Eretz Yisrael, was told to defend his psak the moment he arrived.

II. Two Ways to Read Zos HaPaam — the Rashbam and Rashi

Adam’s first words on seeing Chava are זֹאת הַפַּעַם עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי וּבָשָׂר מִבְּשָׂרִי. What does zos hapaam mean? In English we say “finally,” and that may be the wrong translation entirely.

The Rashbam: “This Time, Only”

Today is the yahrtzeit of the Ri of Dampierre (Rabbeinu Yitzchak, ר״י), regarded as perhaps the greatest of the Rishonim in nigleh. The Rosh writes that when the Rambam argues on the Ri or Rabbeinu Tam, we follow the Ri and Rabbeinu Tam, who were greater in wisdom — an Ashkenazi tradition; the Sefardim have the opposite one. The Ri was a great-grandson of Rashi. Rashi’s daughter Yocheved married Rabbi Meir, and their children were the Rashbam (Shmuel), Rabbeinu Tam (Yaakov), the Rivam (Yitzchak, who died young), and Miriam, mother of the Ri. So the Ri and Rabbeinu Tam were nephew and uncle. Appropriately, the first source comes from the Ri’s uncle.

זֹאת הַפַּעַם עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי — דַּוְקָא, אֲבָל מִכָּאן וָאֵילָךְ אֵינוֹ כֵן, אֶלָּא אַדְּרַבָּה אִיפְּכָא הָאִישׁ יוֹצֵא מִן הָאִשָּׁה. — Rashbam, Bereishis 2:23 (source sheet #3)  3

According to the Rashbam, zos hapaam means “this time — and only this time” a woman comes from a man; from now on it is farkert, man will come from woman. It has nothing to do with “finally I have a mate.” The Rashbam is a pashtan; the story, in his reading, is not primarily the wedding but Adam naming things — how did woman get her name, ishah? Because she was taken from ish. The Sforno learns similarly, and, the Rav noted, so do other Rishonim.

The Rav pressed the obvious question — what is the point of saying “only this time”? Adam needs no one to tell him she came from a man; the Aibershter has just said so. A listener suggested that Adam is saying that although it will never happen again, she will always be called ishah, because the first time she was taken from ish. The Rav allowed the point to stand.

Rashi: “In Contrast to Everything Before”

Rashi (source #4) does not take the Rashbam’s path. He cites the gemara in Yevamos:

זֹאת הַפַּעַם. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא אָדָם עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וְחַיָּה, וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁבָּא עַל חַוָּה (יבמות ס״ג). — Rashi, Bereishis 2:23, citing Yevamos 63a (source #4)  4

On this reading, zos hapaam means “this one is different from everything that came before it”; Adam had been with every animal and was not appeased until Chava. Nitkarerah da’ato (נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ) literally means his mind was cooled. Rashi is quoting the gemara verbatim, and the gemara itself is cited in the name of Rabbi Elazar.4

The Rashbam is Rashi’s grandson; how can he disagree with the gemara? The Rav explained that a drashah does not displace peshuto shel mikra (פְּשׁוּטוֹ שֶׁל מִקְרָא) — there are layers: peshat, remez, drush, sod — and Rashi himself is less of a pashtan than his grandson or the Ibn Ezra. The Ibn Ezra famously said that when he explains Chumash he is giving his own understanding of the text and need not follow the gemara, because he is not ruling on a halachic question. The Maharshal was vehemently opposed, saying this approach created the ground for the Reform; to this day, some academics treat the Ibn Ezra as their hero. The Rebbe, the Rav added, did note that even Rashi occasionally offers a reading that departs from Chazal, and that it is not mutually exclusive.5

But then the real question: what does Rashi mean? Did Adam literally come upon every animal? Lechorah that sounds absurd. Which is where the Maharal comes in.

III. The Maharal’s Problem — Adam Was Already Commanded

The Maharal of Prague (source #4, in the Gur Aryeh) opens with a flat rejection:

שֶׁבָּא עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וכו׳. בְּיִבָּמוֹת פֶּרֶק הַבָּא עַל יְבִמְתּוֹ. וְאֵין הַפֵּירוּשׁ חָלִילָה שֶׁבָּא עֲלֵיהֶם לְמִשְׁכָּב, שֶׁהֲרֵי הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא כְּבָר צִוָּה אוֹתוֹ עַל הָעֲרָיוֹת (סנהדרין נו ע״ב) בַּפָּסוּק “וַיְצַו”. — Maharal, Gur Aryeh, Bereishis 2:23 (source #4)  6

To appreciate his question, the Rav turned to the gemara in Sanhedrin 56b (source #5), which spends several blatt on the seven mitzvos bnei Noach and brings a spread of views about what Adam himself was commanded. One opinion: only avodah zarah. Another adds birkas Hashem, another dinim. The standard view — the Rav’s shorthand — is six of the seven: he was missing eiver min hachai. Why? Because Adam was a vegetarian; he was not permitted to eat meat at all until after the mabul. The Ramban gives the reason: had it not been for Noach, the animals would have been destroyed, and Noach also tended them for a year in the teivah, so he merited dominion. Adam had no such claim.7 Some say Adam was told all seven; Tosafos explains why eiver min hachai would then be relevant at all: if Adam could eat an animal that died on its own, a limb that fell off a living animal would be forbidden even after it died.

The source for the larger view is Rabbi Yochanan’s drashah on the very first pasuk (sheet #1, repeated in #5):

מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי? אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן, דְּאָמַר קְרָא: “וַיְצַו ה׳ אֱלֹקִים עַל הָאָדָם לֵאמֹר מִכֹּל עֵץ הַגָּן אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל.” — וַיְצַו, אֵלּוּ הַדִּינִין … ה׳, זוֹ בִּרְכַּת הַשֵּׁם … אֱלֹקִים, זוֹ עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה … עַל הָאָדָם, זוֹ שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים … לֵאמֹר, זוֹ גִּלּוּי עֲרָיוֹת … מִכֹּל עֵץ הַגָּן, וְלֹא גָּזֵל … אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל, וְלֹא אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי. — Sanhedrin 56b (source #5)  7

The word leimor (לֵאמֹר) alludes to giluy arayos because of the pasuk in Yirmiyah (לֵאמֹר הֵן יְשַׁלַּח אִישׁ אֶת אִשְׁתּוֹ) where the same word introduces a case of relations. And since the prohibition on arayos for bnei Noach includes bestiality according to all opinions, here is the Maharal’s problem: before Adam met any animal, he had been told not to have forbidden relations. Rashi himself (source #5, Rashi on 2:24) says that עַל כֵּן יַעֲזָב אִישׁ is the ruach hakodesh’s way of forbidding arayos to bnei Noach (Sanhedrin 57a).7

A Digression: Yaakov, Two Sisters, and Rabbi Meir

The Rav noted a surprising corollary. Whatever is forbidden to a Jew is forbidden to a gentile, according to the Sages; Rabbi Meir is more lenient: what carries no capital punishment for a Jew, even if it carries kares — like marrying two sisters — is permitted to a goy. This, he said, explains a well-known Rashi. When Yaakov hears Esav is coming, the pasuk says וַיִּירָא יַעֲקֹב מְאֹד וַיֵּצֶר לוֹ, and Rashi says he feared he would be killed, and was distressed lest he kill acherim (אֲחֵרִים), others.8 Who are these acherim? The Rav gave a tradition: in the Mishnayos, Rabbi Meir is referred to as acher; the gemara in Gittin says that Rabbi Meir descended from Nero Caesar, who converted. Nero is of Rome, Rome comes from Esav, so if Yaakov killed Esav, he risked cutting off the line that would produce Rabbi Meir. And why did Yaakov need Rabbi Meir? Because Yaakov married two sisters, and according to the Sages even a gentile may not. Only according to Rabbi Meir was it permitted. (An attendee countered that the acherim are plural; the Rav acknowledged it and moved on.)

The Maharal’s Answer

The Maharal continues with a philosophical exposition (to the point the Rav chose to give in outline): man is the tzurah of everything beneath him in creation, and gives them shleimus. Each tzurah joins with that for which it is the form, and that joining is called biah (בִּיאָה). Adam, as the form of all the lower creatures, joined with all of them in this sense of bringing completion; but this was not a true match, because there was too little in common — it was not the right puzzle piece. Only when Chava came, who is a perfect match for Adam, did he find someone to whom he could bring his completion. That is what biah means here: bringing completion, not physical relations.6

IV. Entering the Animals with the Mind

The Maharal is not alone. The Rav now surveyed a line of authorities who learn Rashi’s biah as mental.

The Maharshal and the Vilna Gaon

The Maharshal (Rabbi Shlomo Luria, מהרש״ל), living in Lublin and a contemporary of the Maharal, writes in his gloss on Rashi (source #6):

אֲחֵרִים שִׁמֵּשׁ. נ״ב: אַף שֶׁאֵין מְשִׁיבִין אֶת הָאֲרִי, מִכָּל מָקוֹם נִרְאֶה לִי דְּלָאו דַּוְקָא שִׁמֵּשׁ, אֶלָּא בָּא בְּמַחֲשָׁבָה. — Maharshal, gloss on Rashi, zos hapaam (source #6)  9

Nichtav nun-beis means “written on the side.” The Maharshal will not “knock off the lion” — Rashi — but it seems to him that Adam did not physically come upon them; he came upon them with his mind, to see whether mating was possible. An attendee observed that Adam, to name the animals, had to plumb their essence, just as Shlomo HaMelech understood the speech of animals; so he could easily assess whether any of them could be a companion, and conclude, as the Maharshal does, that they were on different levels.

The Vilna Gaon, in the Aderes Eliyahu, adds a linguistic point: bo’ al does not need to mean literally; he compares it to the gemara’s phrase about the Kohen Gadol who “came to his cow” (ba etzel parah) on Yom Kippur, which obviously does not mean relations. The Rav noted Chazal use etzel there, not al, so the comparison is not perfect. The Gaon’s sharper point is that the gemara does not really learn this from zos hapaam at all; the source is וּלְאָדָם לֹא מָצָא עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ two pesukim earlier. “He did not find” implies he was searching — and searching does not mean physical contact. If someone says, “I am going to New York to look for a wife,” he does not mean he will be intimate with the city; he means he will look and evaluate, as the gemara in Kiddushin and the Rambam tell us one must see a woman before betrothing her.10

The Torah Temimah, Akeidah, Toldos Yitzchak, and Yalkut Yehoshua

The Torah Temimah (Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein, d. 1941, son of the Aruch HaShulchan) writes (source #8):

רְצוֹנוֹ לוֹמַר שֶׁחָשַׁב וּבִקֵּשׁ לוֹ זוּג מִכָּל הַבְּרִיּוֹת וְלֹא נָחָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁמָּצָא אֶת חַוָּה. וְאִי אֶפְשָׁר לְפָרְשׁוֹ כִּפְשׁוּטוֹ, דְּהֲרֵי מְבוֹאָר בְּעֲבוֹדָה זָרָה כ״ב ב׳ וכו׳ דִּבְהֵמוֹת מִתְעַקֶּרֶת מִבִּיאַת אָדָם, וְאֵלּוּ הַבְּהֵמוֹת וְהַחַיּוֹת הֲלֹא הוֹלִידוּ לְתַכְלִית קִיּוּם הַמִּין. — Torah Temimah, Bereishis 2:23 (source #8)  11

The gemara in Avodah Zarah 22b accepts that an animal can be rendered barren by relations with a human. Had Adam been with every animal, many species would be infertile — yet all the original animals bore young and are here today. Two gemaros contradict; so the one about Adam must not be literal.11 (A listener suggested that perhaps they gave birth beforehand. The Rav said: wait.)

The Akeidah (Rabbi Yitzchak Arama, born in Spain c. 1420, a generation after the pogroms of 1391; he lived to see the expulsion of 1492 and died in Naples two years later) quotes the gemara with an added word: chayah va’of (חַיָּה וָעוֹף) — a bird. The Rav did not know where the bird came from; he suspected a manuscript copyist. The Akeidah (source #9) explains:

יִרְצֶה שֶׁבָּא בְּדַעְתּוֹ וְטוֹב הִתְבּוֹנְנוּתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶם וְעַל טִבְעָם, וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ שֶׁיִּהְיֶה זִוּוּגוֹ וְחִבּוּרוֹ כְּזִוּוּג וְחִבּוּר שׁוּם אֶחָד מֵהֶם. — Akeidas Yitzchak, parshas Bereishis (source #9)  12

The Toldos Yitzchak (source #10) was written by Rabbi Yitzchak Karo, uncle of Rabbi Yosef Karo, who also lived through the Spanish expulsion. He is emphatic: חַס וְשָׁלוֹם שֶׁבָּא בְּפֹעַל. After Adam named the animals through his wisdom, he understood their natures and saw that no female among them agreed with him. He adds that, on peshat, zos hapaam is as the Rashbam says — from now on there will be no need to take a woman from the rib of man.12

The Yalkut Yehoshua (source #11), by Rabbi Yehoshua Rabinowitz, printed in Milwaukee in 1933 — he lived in Brownsville, New York — makes a diyuk in the language:

מֵאָמְרוֹ וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ, כִּי הַמִּפְעָל הַזֶּה מִפְעָל תַּאֲוָנִי הוּא וְלֹא מִפְעָל שִׂכְלִי, וְהָיָה לוֹ לוֹמַר וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה תַּאֲוָתוֹ … אֵין לְפָרֵשׁ כִּפְשׁוּטוֹ. — Yalkut Yehoshua (source #11)  12

If the gemara meant a physical act, it would have said his desire was not appeased (ta’avaso), not his mind (da’ato). Instead, the gemara means he considered the nature and characteristics of every animal and found nothing like himself; then he looked at Chava — at her tichunah and the build of her body — and said: this will work. Why the urgency to find a mate? Because the Aibershter had already blessed him with peru urevu.12 (A discussion followed about whether the bracha of peru urevu in Bereishis 1:28 came before or after Chava’s creation.) Paam itself, the Yalkut says, means a footstep, as in מַה יָּפוּ פְעָמַיִךְ בַּנְּעָלִים, and the Rav told the familiar vort that what the Aibershter treasures about the aliyah laregel is the mud and sweat of the journey in one’s shoes, not the bliss one feels after the shoes come off in the Beis HaMikdash.

So far, then, it looks unanimous: the Maharal, Maharshal, Vilna Gaon, Akeidah, Toldos Yitzchak, Torah Temimah, Yalkut Yehoshua — all against a literal reading. But there are exceptions.

V. The Literal Reading — the Chizkuni and the Maharsha

The Chizkuni (source #12), a thirteenth-century French Rishon, gives the Rashbam’s peshat — and then, in the same breath, takes Rashi at his word:

זֹאת הַפַּעַם עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי. פֵּרֵשׁ רַשִׁ״י מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא אָדָם עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וְחַיָּה וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ. צָרִיךְ לוֹמַר שֶׁנִּתְעַבְּרוּ קֹדֶם שֶׁבָּא עֲלֵיהֶם אָדָם, דְּאִי לָא תֵימָא הָכִי הָא מִיעַקְּרָן בְּבִיאַת אָדָם. — Chizkuni, Bereishis 2:23 (source #12)  13

He accepts the Torah Temimah’s difficulty and answers it: all the animals must already have conceived before Adam came upon them. Afterwards they may have become barren, but the conception had already occurred. The Rav pointed out that the Chizkuni was a Rishon in the thirteenth century, so his kashya is the very kashya that the Torah Temimah would raise centuries later. A listener objected that the Chizkuni offers two peshatim and the second need not be literal; the Rav replied that the Chizkuni says plainly that Adam was with them.13

The Tzeidah LaDerech (Rabbi Yissachar Ber Eilenburg, author of the Be’er Sheva, who wrote a supercommentary on Rashi, source #13) quotes the Chizkuni and rejects it outright: חָלִילָה חָלִילָה לוֹמַר כֵּן — would Adam defile himself in this way? It is not that he actually did anything; as the Toldos Yitzchak says, he came with his mind and was unimpressed. The Tzeidah LaDerech therefore pushes the Chizkuni aside.13 (The Rav added an exchange with the room: if Adam examined all the animals mentally and found them lacking, did it lower his opinion of Hashem — “is this the best You can do?” — and perhaps that is why Hashem wanted him to ask.)

The Maharsha (source #7, on Yevamos 63a, written c. 1600 in Poland) sticks up for the Chizkuni — but, the Rav said, only at the level of logic:

אֵין זֶה מַסְפִּיק, דְּהָא מִשֶּׁנִּתְעַבְּרָה בְּהֵמָה אֵינָהּ מְקַבֶּלֶת זָכָר, וַאֲפִלּוּ זְכַר אָדָם, כְּדִמְוּכָח פֶּרֶק קַמָּא דִבְכוֹרוֹת. וְאֶפְשָׁר לוֹמַר שֶׁנִּתְעַבְּרוּ וְיָלְדוּ בּוֹ בַיּוֹם קֹדֶם שֶׁבָּא עֲלֵיהֶן, דְּהָא חַוָּה נַמִּי יָלְדָה בּוֹ בַיּוֹם כְּדְאָמְרִינַן פֶּרֶק אָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן. — Maharsha, Chiddushei Aggados, Yevamos 63a (source #7)  14

The Chizkuni’s answer is not enough, says the Maharsha: once an animal is pregnant it will not accept a male, as we see in the first perek of Bechoros. So Adam would have had to force each pregnant animal, which makes the story worse. The Maharsha proposes that they conceived and gave birth that very day, before Adam came upon them — for Chava too gave birth the same day, as the gemara in the perek of Adam HaRishon says (Sanhedrin 38b).14 The Rav addressed an objection — the gemara says mishenisaberah, once she is pregnant, not that one pregnancy is complete in a day — with a stern reading: the Chizkuni is still “up the creek,” because the animal’s system would have gone haywire before conception; so if they conceived beforehand they would have refused, and the only logical resolution is that they had already given birth.

Then, the Maharsha adds: וּבַעַל הַנִּצָּחוֹן כָּתַב — the author of the Sefer HaNitzachon writes that the gemara is not literal at all. The Rav asked the class to turn to source #14.

A Manuscript from Prague — the Sefer HaNitzachon

In the late 1300s the Prague Jewish community was a fount of Torah — long before the Maharal, the Shelah, the Tosafos Yom Tov, the Kli Yakar and the Noda BiYehuda — and in those days there were two friends there. One was Avigdor Kara, a philosopher and kabbalist who, according to tradition, made a golem a century and a half before the Maharal. The other was Rabbi Yom Tov Lipmann (Muelhausen). He was summoned to a public disputation; his opponent was a Jew who had converted, Pesach, now called Peter. The Jews thought the debate went well — which was the problem, because the Christians were humiliated; a couple of months later, the Rav said, eighty Jews were killed in Prague. Rabbi Lipmann wrote down the entire debate so that all would know what had happened, and that is the Sefer HaNitzachon (סֵפֶר הַנִּצָּחוֹן).15

וּלְאָדָם לֹא מָצָא עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ. הַכּוֹפְרִים פּוֹקְרִים עַל רַשִׁ״י, שֶׁפֵּרֵשׁ מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה חַיָּה וְעוֹף, וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁנִּזְדַּוְּגָה לוֹ חַוָּה. לָכֵן אוֹמֵר אֲנִי שֶׁאֵין בָּא זֶה לְשׁוֹן תַּשְׁמִישׁ, כִּי הַגָּדוֹל בָּעֲנָקִים אִי אֶפְשָׁר לְשַׁמֵּשׁ עִם עַכְבָּר וְחוֹלְדָה, אֶלָּא אָדָם הַהוֹלֵךְ לְחַפֵּשׂ וּלְבַקֵּשׁ דָּבָר נוֹפֵל עָלָיו לְשׁוֹן בָּא בְּעִבְרִית, כְּמוֹ פְלוֹנִי בָּא עֲלֵיהֶם לְמִלְחָמָה אוֹ בָּא עֲלֵיהֶם בַּעֲלִילוֹת. וְהָכִי פֵּירוּשׁוֹ: לְפִי שֶׁלֹּא הָיָה זוּג לְאָדָם הָלַךְ לְחַפֵּשׂ וְלִבְדּוֹק בְּכָל הַמִּינִים אִם יִמְצָא עוֹד אֶחָד בְּלֹא עֵזֶר, וְכֵיוָן שֶׁלֹּא מָצָא לֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁנִּזְדַּוְּגָה לוֹ חַוָּה. — Sefer HaNitzachon (Rabbi Yom Tov Lipmann-Muelhausen), on Bereishis 2:20 (source #14)  15

Note the opening words: hakofrim pokrim al Rashi — the heretics poke at Rashi. This was a standing problem for Jews in disputations; the Rav noted that Rabbi Yechiel of Paris faced the same Rashi and the same gemara in the 1240s. (Disputations tended to center on Sefer Bereishis, and the Jewish debater had to be baki in it.) The Nitzachon’s answer has two parts. First, a practical one: Adam was a giant — before the sin his head reached the heavens, as the gemara says — and one cannot cohabit with a mouse or a weasel. Second, a linguistic one: bo’ in Hebrew falls on someone who is searching, as in he came against them for war. So the gemara means that, having no mate, Adam went to search among all the species to see if there might be another single one, and since he did not find, his mind was not appeased until Chava was brought to him.

A friendly debate followed in the room about whether “did not find” should have produced chalishus hada’as (dejection) rather than lo nitkarerah; the Rav let the room have its say — it was suggested that Adam wanted to learn whether the concept of being alone exists at all, or that he sought another loner, since he would not break up a couple — and said that either way the point of the Nitzachon is clear. A final question was raised about the Torah being so specific about kashrus and Shabbos and then so oblique here. The Rav’s answer: the Chumash says nothing about this. The extra word is in the gemara’s derivation, from matza or from zos hapaam.

The Levush (Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe, a student of the Rema, d. 1612, author of Levush HaOrah on Rashi), in a source the Rav almost forgot to bring, objects for two reasons. One: there is not enough time in the day for Adam to be with every animal. Two: it is simply repulsive (aklakeit) — even if the commandment of bal teshaktzu (בַּל תְּשַׁקְּצוּ) was given only to Jews, it is a muskal rishon that Adam would not do such a thing.16

The Rav then summarized. By now there are five problems with a literal reading: the Maharal’s (halachic — Adam had been commanded); the Chizkuni and Torah Temimah’s (fertility); the Sefer HaNitzachon’s (Adam’s size); the Levush’s (time); and the Levush’s other (disgust). He then asked for source #15:

וַיֹּאמֶר הָאָדָם זֹאת הַפַּעַם עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי וגו׳. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא אָדָם עַל חַיָּה וּבְהֵמָה, וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁנִּזְדַּוֵּג לְחַוָּה. — Midrash Aggadah (ed. Buber), Bereishis 2:23 (source #15)  16

Solomon Buber, the great researcher of midrash, printed this text about a century ago from manuscript. Note the difference: it has no כָּל — he came upon some animals. This already dissolves the Levush’s first kashya (time). The Rav then fielded a vigorous objection: Rashi and the gemara say kol. If you want to read al literally, kol must go; if you keep kol literally, you must read al as mental. His answer: kol in Chazal often means “quite a lot,” and the Midrash Aggadah suggests the same.16

VI. The Taz — Rashi Means What He Says

Now the Taz (Rabbi David HaLevi Segal, 1586–1667), in Divrei David on Rashi (source #18). The Rav prefaced with a few of the Taz’s stories: that during the Shabsai Tzvi episode he sent his son to observe him and the son returned reporting that he saw nothing alarming other than that he slept on his back; and that during the Chmielnicki massacres, when the Taz, exhausted, fell asleep in shul and dreamed of the pasuk וְגַנּוֹתִי עַל הָעִיר הַזֹּאת לְהוֹשִׁיעָהּ לְמַעֲנִי וּלְמַעַן דָּוִד עַבְדִּי — he took it as a promise for himself, David, told the people to take the old cannon to the weakest section of the wall, and the explosion sent the attackers fleeing. Having established who we are dealing with, he turned to the text.

זֹאת הַפַּעַם, מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וְחַיָּה. בְּפֶרֶק הַבָּא עַל יְבִמְתּוֹ … וְהִקְשׁוּ הַמְפָרְשִׁים הֵיאַךְ בָּא עַל בְּהֵמָה, שֶׁהֲרֵי נִצְטַוּוּ עַל הָעֲרָיוֹת וּבְהֵמָה בִּכְלַל עֲרָיוֹת, כְּדִתְנַן פֶּרֶק אַרְבַּע מִיתוֹת … וְדָבָר זֶה אֵינוֹ עוֹלֶה לְפֵירוּשׁ רַשִׁ״י שֶׁכָּתַב בְּהֶדְיָא בְּיִבָּמוֹת שֶׁשִּׁמֵּשׁ פְּעָמִים אֲחֵרִים … וּבֶאֱמֶת הִקְשׁוּ קוּשְׁיָא זוֹ מֵחִסָּרוֹן יְדִיעָה, דְּאִיתָא פֶּרֶק אָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן (דַּף לַ״ח). — Taz, Divrei David on Rashi, Bereishis 2:23 (source #18; abridged, print faint)  17

The Taz says: the commentators asked how Adam could have come upon an animal when he was already commanded on arayos — and therefore explained it as mental. A nice peshat, says the Taz, but not Rashi’s. Rashi in Yevamos writes explicitly that shimeish pe’amim acheiros — Adam cohabited at other times. (The Rav added that some say this was Lilis, and that the Ibn Ezra brings those who read zos hapaam as a contrast to Lilis. An attendee insisted that Rashi’s wording is general, not about Lilis, and the Rav agreed that the gemara in context concerns the animals.)

The Taz then says: those who ask this question lack knowledge (מֵחִסָּרוֹן יְדִיעָה) of the gemara in Sanhedrin 38b. The Rav put source #19 on the table:

אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בַּר חֲנִינָא: שְׁתֵּים עֶשְׂרֵה שָׁעוֹת הָיָה הַיּוֹם. שָׁעָה רִאשׁוֹנָה הוּצְבַּר עֲפָרוֹ, שְׁנִיָּה נַעֲשָׂה גּוֹלֶם, שְׁלִישִׁית נִמְתְּחוּ אֵבָרָיו, רְבִיעִית נִזְרְקָה בּוֹ נְשָׁמָה, חֲמִישִׁית עָמַד עַל רַגְלָיו, שִׁשִּׁית קָרָא שֵׁמוֹת, שְׁבִיעִית נִזְדַּוְּגָה לוֹ חַוָּה, שְׁמִינִית עָלוּ לַמִּטָּה שְׁנַיִם וְיָרְדוּ אַרְבָּעָה, תְּשִׁיעִית נִצְטַוָּה שֶׁלֹּא לֶאֱכֹל מִן הָאִילָן, עֲשִׂירִית סָרַח, אַחַת עֶשְׂרֵה נִדּוֹן, שְׁתֵּים עֶשְׂרֵה נִטְרַד וְהָלַךְ לוֹ, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: “וְאָדָם בִּיקָר בַּל יָלִין.” — Sanhedrin 38b (source #19)  18

The sixth hour: Adam gives names. The seventh: Chava is joined to him. The eighth: two go up to bed and four come down. The ninth: נִצְטַוָּה שֶׁלֹּא לֶאֱכֹל מִן הָאִילָן — he was commanded about the Tree. Tosafos (source #20) adds that the four were Adam, Chava, Kayin and Kayin’s twin sister, because Hevel was born later, and Bereishis Rabbah derives from אֶת אָחִיו אֶת הָבֶל that Hevel had two twin sisters.18 The point for our sugya: the pesukim are out of order. In the Torah the command וַיְצַו comes before the naming, but the gemara places Adam’s commands after Chava. So the prohibition of bestiality was not yet in force when Adam spent his hour of searching. Rashi’s own source for arayos for bnei Noach is the later verse, עַל כֵּן יַעֲזָב אִישׁ אֶת אָבִיו וְאֶת אִמּוֹ וְדָבַק בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ (#5 above, Rashi on 2:24), which is certainly after Chava. The Maharal’s question evaporates.17

The Taz continues with the remaining difficulties. The Levush’s revulsion: before the sin, every animal was beautiful — the nachash stood on two feet and spoke; the Ibn Ezra brings the view that it literally spoke. The animals were created by the word of Hashem; only after the sin did they, too, partake. His closing instruction: do not remove the words of the Sages from their simple meaning. The fertility question is answered by the Chizkuni (they conceived) and Maharsha (they gave birth); the Sefer HaNitzachon and Levush’s practical objections are answered by the Midrash Aggadah — some animals. The Rav summed up the scorecard: all five questions are answered; no one comes out wounded.

The Room Pushes Back

A lively exchange followed, and the Rav made sure it was in the write-up. Is it plausible that one hour sufficed for even a few? Someone said even three would be a chazakah. Why would he need to try, being a scientist who understood their depth? — because there were no animals like him; and if animals looked quite different then, the Midrash that the nachash fathered Kayin (not the view of the gemara in Sanhedrin) shows how little we know about the creatures. The Kabbalists, the Rav added, hold that the animals lost their knowledge of Hashem when the sin occurred — lo yare’u velo yashchisu (לֹא יָרֵעוּ וְלֹא יַשְׁחִיתוּ) will come true when Moshiach restores it — and that this is a spiritual loss, unlike the nachash’s physical one.

What was Adam looking for — a mind or a body? The room divided. The Rav’s own view: both, as in any shidduch. Ezer kenegdo (עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ) is not only peru urevu but a whole companionship; and Rashi on 2:18 says zachah — ezer; lo zachah — kenegdo lehilachem. The Netziv of Volozhin adds that the greatest ezer is a wife who is kenegdo: one who pushes her husband to do more, not a yes-woman.2 And the Rav insisted the word da’ato shows he was looking for more than physical compatibility; for the bodily alone he could have made a golem.

VII. The Last Word — an Argument Still Open

Where do we stand? On one side, the Maharal, Maharshal, Vilna Gaon, Akeidah, Toldos Yitzchak, Torah Temimah, Yalkut Yehoshua, the Tzeidah LaDerech, the Sefer HaNitzachon and the Levush — all agreeing that Rashi cannot mean it literally, for a series of different reasons. On the other, the Chizkuni (who takes it literally and answers the fertility problem), the Maharsha (who defends him on logic), and the Taz, who defends the literal reading of Rashi with a full answer to every question. A listener summed up the standing of the debate: “It seems like it’s everyone against Rashi.” The Rav’s answer was that Rashi is quoting the gemara verbatim, and the question is only what the gemara meant; and that the Taz’s point stands: Rashi himself in Yevamos is explicit.

The Rav was about to turn to the Yalkut Yehoshua’s diyuk on Rashi’s own wording in Yevamos — that Rashi there says meshamesh — when the recording breaks off. The write-up stops where the recording stops.

7. סנהדרין נו ע״ב (sheet #5); רש״י בראשית ב:כד (sheet #5); רמב״ן; תוספות. Sanhedrin 56b confirmed on Sefaria: Rabbi Yochanan derives the seven mitzvos from Bereishis 2:16, with לֵאמֹר tied to giluy arayos via Yirmiyah 3:1; the same sugya records that Rabbi Yehudah says Adam was commanded only about idolatry. Rashi on 2:24 confirmed: עַל כֵּן יַעֲזָב אִישׁ, רוּחַ הַקֹּדֶשׁ אוֹמֶרֶת כֵּן לֶאֱסֹר עַל בְּנֵי נֹחַ הָעֲרָיוֹת (סנהדרין נ״ז); the following Rashi, לְבָשָׂר אֶחָד, cites Sanhedrin 58 for the child.
Flag: The Ramban’s reason that Adam did not eat meat (Noach’s merit in preserving the animals), and the Tosafos on eiver min hachai (a limb from an animal that died of itself), were given orally without a location. Not verified; likely Ramban on Bereishis 9:3 and Tosafos on Sanhedrin 56b, but unconfirmed.
8. רש״י בראשית לב:ח; גיטין נו ע״א; Rabbi Meir and the Noahide. Confirmed on Sefaria. Rashi on וַיִּירָא יַעֲקֹב מְאֹד וַיֵּצֶר לוֹ: וַיִּירָא שֶׁמָּא יֵהָרֵג, וַיֵּצֶר לוֹ אִם יַהֲרֹג הוּא אֶת אֲחֵרִים. Gittin 56a: Nero (נֵירוֹן קֵיסָר) fled, converted, וּנְפַק מִינֵּיהּ רַבִּי מֵאִיר.
Flag: Rashi himself does not identify the acherim with Rabbi Meir; that is a tradition the Rav cited, and the Rav also invoked a machlokes between the Sages and Rabbi Meir on whether a Noahide may marry two sisters, without a source. Both remain unverified. Also: the gemara says only that Rabbi Meir descended from Nero; the Rav’s “great-grandson” is not in the text.
9. מהרש״ל, gloss on Rashi (sheet #6). Rabbi Shlomo Luria (Lublin, 16th c.). The sheet reads: אֲחֵרִים שִׁמֵּשׁ. נ״ב: אַף שֶׁאֵין מְשִׁיבִין אֶת הָאֲרִי, מִכָּל מָקוֹם נִרְאֶה לִי דְּלָאו דַּוְקָא שִׁמֵּשׁ אֶלָּא בָּא בְּמַחֲשָׁבָה. Nichtav nun-beis is read by the Rav as nichtav betzido, a marginal note.
Flag: The title of the Maharshal’s work in which this gloss appears is not given on the sheet. Not located on Sefaria; no work is named here in order not to guess.
10. גר״א, אדרת אליהו; קידושין; רמב״ם. The Vilna Gaon, as presented by the Rav (not on the sheet): bo’ al need not be literal, as with the Kohen Gadol who “came to his cow” on Yom Kippur; and the true source of the gemara is וּלְאָדָם לֹא מָצָא — searching. The Rav compares the need to see a bride before betrothal (Kiddushin and the Rambam).
Flag: No locations were given for any of these. Likely candidates (Aderes Eliyahu on Bereishis; Kiddushin 41a; Rambam Hilchos Ishus 3:19; the Mishnah in Yoma on the parah) were not verified and are not asserted.
11. תורה תמימה בראשית ב:כג (sheet #8); עבודה זרה כב ע״ב. Rabbi Baruch HaLevi Epstein (1860–1941), son of the Aruch HaShulchan. Avodah Zarah 22b confirmed on Sefaria: Rav Tachlifa in the name of Rav Sheila bar Avina in the name of Rav says a gentile spares his animal so it will not become barren. The sheet’s Torah Temimah cites ע״ז כ״ב ב׳.
Flag: The Torah Temimah does not appear as a separate work on Sefaria (checked: no book named Torah Temimah on Genesis), so his text is given as printed on the sheet.
12. עקידת יצחק (sheet #9); תולדות יצחק (#10); ילקוט יהושע (#11). Akeidah: Rabbi Yitzchak Arama (c. 1420–1494). The sheet quotes: חַיָּה וְעוֹף וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ בָּהֶם (יבמות ס״ג). יִרְצֶה שֶׁבָּא בְּדַעְתּוֹ וְטוֹב הִתְבּוֹנְנוּתוֹ עֲלֵיהֶם וְעַל טִבְעָם. Toldos Yitzchak: Rabbi Yitzchak Karo, uncle of the Mechaber, on zos hapaam. Yalkut Yehoshua: Rabbi Yehoshua Rabinowitz; the Rav said it was printed in Milwaukee in 1933.
Flag: Texts reproduced from the sheet; none of the three was located on Sefaria. The addition of of (bird) to Rashi’s wording (chayah va’of) is the Akeidah’s on the sheet, though it also appears in the Sefer HaNitzachon. The publication details of the Yalkut Yehoshua are from the Rav and unverified.
13. חזקוני בראשית ב:כג (sheet #12); צידה לדרך (sheet #13). Chizkuni confirmed on Sefaria: פֵּרֵשׁ רַשִׁ״י מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא אָדָם עַל כָּל בְּהֵמָה וְחַיָּה וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ. צָרִיךְ לוֹמַר שֶׁנִּתְעַבְּרוּ קֹדֶם שֶׁבָּא עֲלֵיהֶם אָדָם דְּאִי לָא תֵימָא הָכִי הָא מִיעַקְּרָן בְּבִיאַת אָדָם. Rabbi Chizkiyah ben Manoach, 13th-century France. The Tzeidah LaDerech is the supercommentary on Rashi by Rabbi Yissachar Ber Eilenburg, author of Be’er Sheva, which quotes the Chizkuni and rejects the literal reading as חָלִילָה חָלִילָה.
Flag: The Tzeidah LaDerech was not located on Sefaria; text as on the sheet.
14. מהרש״א, חידושי אגדות יבמות סג ע״א (sheet #7). Confirmed on Sefaria (Chidushei Agadot on Yevamot 63a). Quotes the Chizkuni, says אֵין זֶה מַסְפִּיק, דְּהָא מִשֶּׁנִּתְעַבְּרָה בְּהֵמָה אֵינָהּ מְקַבֶּלֶת זָכָר … כְּדִמְוּכָח פֶּרֶק קַמָּא דִבְכוֹרוֹת, proposes שֶׁנִּתְעַבְּרוּ וְיָלְדוּ בּוֹ בַיּוֹם, citing the perek אָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן for Chava giving birth the same day (Sanhedrin 38b, confirmed), and then וּבַעַל הַנִּצָּחוֹן כָּתַב. Rabbi Shmuel Eliezer Eidels (1555–1631).
Flag: The Maharsha’s proof-text that a pregnant animal will not accept a male is given as perek kama of Bechoros. Bechoros 8a was checked and does not contain it, and the exact daf was not found. Left unresolved.
15. סֵפֶר הַנִּצָּחוֹן (sheet #14). Rabbi Yom Tov Lipmann-Muelhausen of Prague (late 14th–early 15th c.), record of a disputation against the apostate Peter (formerly Pesach). The sheet’s text begins וּלְאָדָם לֹא מָצָא עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ. הַכּוֹפְרִים פּוֹקְרִים עַל רַשִׁ״י and compares bo’ to בָּא עֲלֵיהֶם לְמִלְחָמָה. Its claim that Adam was too large is supported by Sanhedrin 38b (confirmed on Sefaria): Adam spanned the world until sin diminished him.
Flag: The Rav’s historical details — the identity of the disputant, the timing of the debate, the Avigdor Kara golem tradition, the Rabbi Yechiel of Paris disputation of the 1240s, and especially the number of Jews killed afterward (the Rav said eighty) — are oral and unverified. Do not cite the figure.
16. מדרש אגדה (ed. Buber; sheet #15); לבוש. The sheet text: מְלַמֵּד שֶׁבָּא אָדָם עַל חַיָּה וּבְהֵמָה, וְלֹא נִתְקָרְרָה דַעְתּוֹ עַד שֶׁנִּזְדַּוֵּג לְחַוָּה — without kol. Edited by Solomon Buber. The Levush (Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe, d. 1612, student of the Rema) objects on the grounds of insufficient time and the repulsiveness of the act, which is not covered by bal teshaktzu.
Flag: The Levush is not on the source sheet and no location was given; the title Levush HaOrah is the Rav’s. Not located on Sefaria.
17. ט״ז, דברי דוד (sheet #18); סנהדרין לח ע״א (sheet #17). Rabbi David HaLevi Segal (1586–1667). The sheet text is printed faintly; what is legible runs וְהִקְשׁוּ הַמְפָרְשִׁים הֵיאַךְ בָּא עַל בְּהֵמָה, שֶׁהֲרֵי נִצְטַוּוּ עַל הָעֲרָיוֹת … וּבֶאֱמֶת הִקְשׁוּ קוּשְׁיָא זוֹ מֵחִסָּרוֹן יְדִיעָה. Sheet #17, a baraita that Adam was created on Erev Shabbos, is cited from the sheet (placed by the sheet at Sanhedrin 38a, immediately before Rabbi Yochanan bar Chanina’s twelve hours in 38b).
Flag: The Taz was not located on Sefaria, so the Hebrew quoted is as legible on the sheet; the Rav’s paraphrase of the five answers is oral. The Taz biography (the Shabsai Tzvi report, the Chmielnicki dream) is told as maaseh without source. Sanhedrin 38a was not directly fetched; 38b was.
18. סנהדרין לח ע״ב (sheet #19); תוספות (sheet, unnumbered). Confirmed on Sefaria. Rabbi Yochanan bar Chanina: six hours קָרָא שֵׁמוֹת, seventh נִזְדַּוְּגָה לוֹ חַוָּה, eighth עָלוּ לַמִּטָּה שְׁנַיִם וְיָרְדוּ אַרְבָּעָה, ninth נִצְטַוָּה שֶׁלֹּא לֶאֱכֹל מִן הָאִילָן, tenth סָרַח, and אָדָם בִּיקָר בַּל יָלִין (Tehillim 49:13). The unnumbered excerpt at the foot of the sheet comments on וְיָרְדוּ אַרְבָּעָה and cites Bereishis Rabbah on אֶת אָחִיו אֶת הָבֶל.
Flag: The unnumbered excerpt is labeled only by its position and its content (Tosafos on Sanhedrin 38b, per the Rav); the printed source label is not legible. Not verified against Tosafos.
